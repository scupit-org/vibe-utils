Guide 14 — Shader math: twinkles and time

Part of the web-3d-panel-navigation math curriculum. You just finished guide 13 (GLSL primer, smoothstep falloff). This guide stays in fragment-shader territory, but the math we care about now is what makes a starfield feel like a sky rather than a grid of dots: phase, frequency, color tint distribution, and additive blending.

1. Why this matters

Open src/skybox/starfield-skybox.ts. The fragment shader at lines 53–61 does almost no math, but every line carries a deliberate choice:

// starfield-skybox.ts:54-60
vec2 centered = gl_PointCoord - vec2(0.5);
float dist = length(centered);
float falloff = smoothstep(0.5, 0.1, dist);
float twinkle = 0.7 + 0.3 * sin(uTime * uTwinkleFreq + vPhase);
float intensity = vBrightness * twinkle * falloff;
vec3 color = mix(uColorWarm, uColorCool, vTint);
gl_FragColor = vec4(color * intensity, intensity);

Three things combine to make this feel like a sky:

  • Phase — line 57 adds a per-star phase vPhase inside the sine. Without it, every star pulses on the same beat and the whole field strobes like a christmas tree.
  • Power-law tint — line 89 sets tints[i] = Math.pow(Math.random(), 1.8). Most stars get a tint near zero (warm); a few get a tint near one (cool). A 50/50 mix would look painted; a biased mix looks observed.
  • Additive blending — line 124 sets blending: THREE.AdditiveBlending. Where two stars overlap, light adds. That is how real emissive light behaves; the over-paint default is wrong for stars.

None of these tricks are deep. But understanding why each one is the right knob to turn is the whole point of this guide.

2. Building intuition

2a. Phase

A sine wave has the same shape no matter where you start sampling it. Shifting the input by a constant slides the wave horizontally — that constant is the phase:

$$\sin(\omega t + \varphi) \;=\; \sin\bigl(\omega(t + \varphi/\omega)\bigr).$$

Different stars get different $\varphi$ values. They all oscillate at the same frequency $\omega$, but they cross zero, peak, and trough at different times. The result is incoherent shimmer instead of a single pulse.

Six sine waves with the same $\omega$ but six different $\varphi$ values, stacked. Vertical line shows "now." Each star samples its row at this line.

2b. Power law

Take a uniform random number $u \in [0, 1)$ and raise it to a power $p$. If $p = 1$, nothing changes — the output is still uniform. If $p > 1$, you bend the function downward in the middle: most $u$ values map to small outputs.

Left: $f(u) = u^p$ for several $p$. Right: histogram of $u^p$ for $u \sim \text{Uniform}[0,1)$. As $p$ grows, mass piles up near zero.

2c. Additive blending

Two overlapping circles. With over-paint, the top circle hides the bottom where they cross. With additive, the colors add, and the overlap is brighter. Stars are emissive: photons from two stars add at the sensor, they do not occlude each other.

Left: over-paint. Middle: additive. Right: same additive at lower intensity, showing that overlap reads as a brighter region rather than a hidden shape.

3. The math

3a. Sinusoidal phase modulation

Define the project's twinkle expression as

$$\text{twinkle}(t) \;=\; b \;+\; a \cdot \sin(\omega\, t + \varphi),$$

with

  • $b$ = base intensity = 0.7 in the shader,
  • $a$ = amplitude = 0.3,
  • $\omega$ = angular frequency = the uniform uTwinkleFreq, default 1.5 rad/s (line 21: const DEFAULT_TWINKLE_FREQ = 1.5),
  • $\varphi$ = per-star phase, drawn once at construction.

Because $\sin$ is bounded in $[-1, 1]$,

$$\text{twinkle}(t) \;\in\; [\,b - a,\; b + a\,] \;=\; [0.4, \; 1.0].$$

That bound is the design choice. A star never goes fully dark (it never drops to $0$) and it never overshoots 1.0. The minimum brightness 0.4 is what keeps the field feeling alive when paused — even at the trough, every star is visibly emitting.

Units check. $\omega$ is in radians per second because uTime is in seconds (see starfield-skybox.ts:149: uTime.value = elapsed * 0.001). The perceptual frequency in cycles per second is

$$f \;=\; \frac{\omega}{2\pi} \;\approx\; \frac{1.5}{6.283} \;\approx\; 0.239 \text{ Hz},$$

so an individual star completes a full bright-dim-bright cycle roughly every 4.2 seconds. Slow enough that you don't notice any single star, fast enough that the field as a whole is always moving.

3b. Per-particle phase

Without per-star phase, every star evaluates $\sin(\omega t + \varphi_0)$ for the same $\varphi_0$. That means at any time $t$, every star has the same twinkle value. The field brightens and dims as one body. Wrong.

The fix: at construction, give each star a uniformly random phase in $[0, 2\pi]$:

// starfield-skybox.ts:85
phases[i] = Math.random() * Math.PI * 2;

Stored as a per-vertex attribute aPhase, passed through to the fragment shader as vPhase:

// starfield-skybox.ts:25, 28, 35
attribute float aPhase;
varying float vPhase;
// ...
vPhase = aPhase;

The full population of stars is now decorrelated. At any given $t$, the twinkle values across all stars are spread across $[0.4, 1.0]$ uniformly (well, sinusoidally weighted; see exercise 5).

3c. Why $\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$ and not $\sin(\omega t)\cos(\varphi)$?

It is tempting to factor: surely $\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$ "expands" to something with $\cos(\varphi)$ in it? Yes — the angle-sum identity gives

$$\sin(\omega t + \varphi) \;=\; \sin(\omega t)\cos(\varphi) \;+\; \cos(\omega t)\sin(\varphi).$$

If you accidentally drop the second term and use only $\sin(\omega t)\cos(\varphi)$, you get a standing wave: every star still crosses zero at the same instant ($\omega t = 0, \pi, 2\pi, \dots$), and only the amplitude varies per star. The field still pulses in unison, just with different individual loudnesses. That is exactly the failure mode we were trying to avoid.

The additive form $\omega t + \varphi$ keeps the wave shape and shifts it in time. The multiplicative form $\sin(\omega t)\cos(\varphi)$ keeps the timing and rescales the amplitude. We want shifted timing.

3d. Power-law color biasing

Mixing warm and cool 50/50 (uniform tint) gives an evenly purple-pink field. The eye reads it as artificial. A real night sky is almost entirely warm-white with a few standout cool blue stars.

The trick on line 89:

// starfield-skybox.ts:87-89
// Bias the palette toward the warm end — a few cool stars stand out
// more than a 50/50 mix would.
tints[i] = Math.pow(Math.random(), 1.8);

Then in the shader (line 59): color = mix(uColorWarm, uColorCool, vTint). So tint = 0 means pure warm, tint = 1 means pure cool, and the bias squashes most stars to the warm end.

Inverse-CDF derivation. Let $U \sim \text{Uniform}[0, 1)$ and $X = U^p$ with $p > 0$. We want the distribution of $X$. The CDF of $X$ is

$$F_X(x) \;=\; P(X \leq x) \;=\; P(U^p \leq x) \;=\; P\bigl(U \leq x^{1/p}\bigr) \;=\; x^{1/p}, \qquad x \in [0, 1].$$

Differentiating gives the density

$$f_X(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{p} \, x^{\,1/p - 1}, \qquad x \in (0, 1].$$

For $p > 1$, the exponent $1/p - 1$ is negative, so $f_X(x) \to \infty$ as $x \to 0^+$ — mass piles up near zero. With $p = 1.8$:

$$f_X(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{1.8} \, x^{\,1/1.8 - 1} \;=\; 0.\overline{5} \cdot x^{-0.444\dots},$$

and the median is the $x$ where $F_X(x) = 0.5$, i.e.

$$x^{1/1.8} = 0.5 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 0.5^{1.8} \;\approx\; 0.287.$$

So half of all stars have a tint $\leq 0.287$ — meaning more than half of the field is mixed toward the warm end by at least 71% warm-to-29%-cool.

3e. Additive blending math

The OpenGL blending equation is

$$C_{\text{out}} \;=\; C_{\text{src}} \cdot s_{\text{src}} \;+\; C_{\text{dst}} \cdot s_{\text{dst}},$$

where $s_{\text{src}}$ and $s_{\text{dst}}$ are the source and destination blend factors. Two common configurations:

Mode$s_{\text{src}}$$s_{\text{dst}}$Equation
Over-paint$\alpha_{\text{src}}$$1 - \alpha_{\text{src}}$$C = C_s\alpha_s + C_d(1 - \alpha_s)$
Additive$1$$1$$C = C_s + C_d$

The output is then clamped per channel to $[0, 1]$ by the framebuffer (or kept HDR if you set up a float framebuffer; we don't here).

Three.js's THREE.AdditiveBlending sets the source factor to SrcAlpha and the destination factor to One, so technically the equation is

$$C_{\text{out}} \;=\; C_{\text{src}} \cdot \alpha_{\text{src}} \;+\; C_{\text{dst}}.$$

And because the fragment shader writes

gl_FragColor = vec4(color * intensity, intensity);

the alpha is the same as the intensity. So when the GPU multiplies color * intensity by intensity as the source factor, you actually get a slight quadratic falloff on the contribution. That is fine — and useful: the dimmer a star, the less it pollutes its neighbors. Bright stars dominate. Dim ones lay quiet contribution.

Why additive is right for stars: light from two emissive sources at the same image pixel really does add. A camera sensor counts photons; two stars in the same pixel deposit roughly twice as many. Over-paint, by contrast, asks "which one is on top" — but stars are not occluding each other; they are co-emitting.

3f. Putting it together

The final per-pixel intensity for a star sprite is

$$I(t, \mathrm{p}) \;=\; \underbrace{b_{\text{star}}}_{\substack{\text{vBrightness}\\ \text{[0.5,1]}}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\bigl(0.7 + 0.3 \sin(\omega t + \varphi)\bigr)}_{\text{twinkle}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\mathrm{smoothstep}(0.5, 0.1, \lVert \mathrm{p} - \tfrac{1}{2}\rVert)}_{\text{falloff (guide 13)}},$$

where $\mathrm{p}$ is gl_PointCoord. The final color is

$$\mathbf{c}_{\text{final}} \;=\; \operatorname{mix}(\text{warm}, \text{cool}, \text{tint}) \cdot I,$$

and the fragment writes vec4(c_final, I). The framebuffer adds it on top of whatever was there before, so overlapping stars build glow.

4. In this codebase

Four snippets, walked through.

4a. The twinkle expression — line 57

float twinkle = 0.7 + 0.3 * sin(uTime * uTwinkleFreq + vPhase);

Direct translation of $b + a \sin(\omega t + \varphi)$. uTime is updated every frame from elapsed * 0.001 (line 149), converting milliseconds to seconds. uTwinkleFreq is the $\omega$ uniform, defaulting to 1.5. vPhase is the per-star $\varphi$, varying-passed from the vertex shader.

4b. Power-law tint — line 89

// starfield-skybox.ts:85-89
phases[i] = Math.random() * Math.PI * 2;
brightnesses[i] = 0.5 + Math.random() * 0.5;
// Bias the palette toward the warm end — a few cool stars stand out
// more than a 50/50 mix would.
tints[i] = Math.pow(Math.random(), 1.8);

Per star, three random draws, all uniform in $[0,1)$ but transformed differently:

  • Phase: scaled to $[0, 2\pi)$. Uniform.
  • Brightness: shifted to $[0.5, 1.0)$. Uniform on its sub-range — every star gets at least 50% brightness so dim stars are still visible, but bright stars stand out 2× more.
  • Tint: raised to the 1.8 power. Skewed toward 0 (warm).

4c. Additive material — lines 118–124

// starfield-skybox.ts:118-124
const starMaterial = new THREE.ShaderMaterial({
  vertexShader: STARFIELD_VERTEX_SHADER,
  fragmentShader: STARFIELD_FRAGMENT_SHADER,
  transparent: true,
  depthWrite: false,
  depthTest: false,
  blending: THREE.AdditiveBlending,

Three settings cooperate:

  • transparent: true tells Three.js to consider this material's alpha output and to render it in the transparent pass.
  • depthWrite: false means stars don't write to the depth buffer. If they did, a near-transparent star pixel would still occlude things behind it.
  • depthTest: false means stars don't read from the depth buffer either — relevant because the skybox is conceptually behind everything; we always want stars to draw.
  • blending: THREE.AdditiveBlending selects the additive equation we derived in 3e.

4d. Per-star phase attribute setup

// starfield-skybox.ts:75, 85, 94 — collected
const phases = new Float32Array(count);
// ...
phases[i] = Math.random() * Math.PI * 2;
// ...
geometry.setAttribute("aPhase", new THREE.BufferAttribute(phases, 1));

One float per star, attached to the geometry as aPhase. The vertex shader reads it as an attribute, then writes it to a varying so the fragment shader can read it. There is no per-frame work involved: phases are drawn once at construction and never touched again. The animation comes entirely from uTime changing each frame.

6. Worked examples

Example 1 — Twinkle at a moment in time

Given $\omega = 1.5$, $\varphi = \pi/4$, $b = 0.7$, $a = 0.3$, $t = 2.5\,\text{s}$. Compute $\text{twinkle}(t)$.

The argument is

$$\omega t + \varphi \;=\; 1.5 \cdot 2.5 + \pi/4 \;=\; 3.75 + 0.7854 \;\approx\; 4.5354 \text{ rad}.$$

And

$$\sin(4.5354) \;\approx\; -0.9852.$$

So

$$\text{twinkle} \;\approx\; 0.7 + 0.3 \cdot (-0.9852) \;\approx\; 0.7 - 0.2956 \;\approx\; 0.404.$$

Near the trough — this star is at its dimmest right now (lower bound is $0.4$). Sanity check: the next time it hits exactly $0.4$ is when $\sin = -1$, i.e. $\omega t + \varphi = 3\pi/2 + 2\pi k$. Solving for the smallest $t > 2.5$: $1.5 t = 3\pi/2 - \pi/4 = 5\pi/4 \approx 3.927$, so $t \approx 2.618\,\text{s}$.

Example 2 — Fraction of warm-tinted stars

With $p = 1.8$, what fraction of stars have $\text{tint} \leq 0.3$?

From section 3d, the CDF is $F_X(x) = x^{1/p}$, so

$$P(\text{tint} \leq 0.3) \;=\; 0.3^{\,1/1.8} \;=\; 0.3^{0.5556}.$$

Computing: $\ln(0.3) \approx -1.2040$, multiplied by $0.5556$ gives $-0.6689$, exponentiated gives $\approx 0.512$.

So about 51% of stars are at least 70% warm. Compare this to $p = 1.0$ (no bias), where $P(\text{tint} \leq 0.3) = 0.3 = 30\%$.

Example 3 — Effective frequency in Hz

If you wanted a star to complete a full cycle every 10 seconds, what value of uTwinkleFreq should you pass?

One full cycle is $2\pi$ radians. Ten seconds means $\omega \cdot 10 = 2\pi$, so $\omega = \pi/5 \approx 0.628$. Pass twinkleFreq: 0.628 in StarfieldSkyboxOptions. (The default 1.5 corresponds to ~4.2 second cycles, as derived in section 3a.)

9. Appendix: Solutions

Show solutions

1. At $t = 0$: $\sin(0 + \pi) = 0$, so twinkle = $0.7$. At $t = \pi/3$: argument is $1.5 \cdot \pi/3 + \pi = \pi/2 + \pi = 3\pi/2$. $\sin(3\pi/2) = -1$, so twinkle = $0.7 - 0.3 = 0.4$. (Trough.)

2. The midpoint of $[0.2, 1.0]$ is $0.6$, so $b = 0.6$. The half-range is $0.4$, so $a = 0.4$.

3. The PDF of $X = U^p$ is $f_X(x) = (1/p) x^{1/p - 1}$. For $p > 1$, the exponent $1/p - 1$ is negative, so $f_X$ blows up as $x \to 0^+$ — probability density is concentrated near zero. Equivalently, the CDF $F_X(x) = x^{1/p}$ has $F_X(0.5) > 0.5$ when $p > 1$ (e.g. $0.5^{1/1.8} \approx 0.674$), meaning more than half of all probability mass falls below $x = 0.5$.

4. $\sin(\omega t)\cos(\varphi) = 0$ iff $\sin(\omega t) = 0$ or $\cos(\varphi) = 0$. The second is independent of $t$; for any $\varphi$ with $\cos\varphi \neq 0$, the zeros are at $\omega t = k\pi$ — the same set for every star. Whereas $\sin(\omega t + \varphi) = 0$ iff $\omega t + \varphi = k\pi$, i.e. $t = (k\pi - \varphi)/\omega$, which depends on $\varphi$.

5. The twinkle value at fixed $t$ is $b + a\sin(\Theta)$ where $\Theta = \omega t + \varphi$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$ (since $\varphi$ is uniform and adding a constant to a uniform circular variable is still uniform). The distribution of $\sin(\Theta)$ for $\Theta$ uniform on a full period is the arcsine distribution on $[-1, 1]$ — U-shaped, with peaks at $\pm 1$. So twinkle values pile up near $b - a$ and $b + a$ (the extremes) and are sparse near $b$ (the middle). Counterintuitive but visible if you stare at the field: at any moment, more stars are near full brightness or near minimum than are halfway in between.

6. With $p = 1$, tint is uniform. The field will look more evenly purple — half cool, half warm. The "cool stars stand out" effect goes away because being cool is no longer rare.

7. New range is $[0.7 - 0.6, 0.7 + 0.6] = [0.1, 1.3]$. Two problems: (a) the upper bound exceeds 1.0, so bright stars get clipped — many stars will pin at the framebuffer max for long stretches, losing nuance; (b) the lower bound 0.1 is so dim that stars effectively flicker out and back, which reads as broken/flashing rather than alive.

8. Bound: $|a_1| + |a_2|$ — the worst case is both sines at their extremes simultaneously. To stay in $[0, 1]$ with $b = 0.7$ you need $|a_1| + |a_2| \leq \min(0.7, 0.3) = 0.3$. Pick e.g. $a_1 = 0.2$, $a_2 = 0.1$, $\omega_1 = 1.5$, $\omega_2 = 4.7$ for a slow base wobble plus a faster shimmer.

9. Use $1 - u^{1.8}$, or equivalently $1 - \text{Math.pow(Math.random(), 1.8)}$. The CDF mirrors. Or, equivalently, swap warm and cool in the mix call. (The two are mathematically the same outcome.)
