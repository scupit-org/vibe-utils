Guide 11 — Hinge Tiling Capstone

Capstone for the panel-positioning track. Companion to src/zoom-plane-parser.ts, lines 355–496.

1. Why this matters

This guide is the math companion to calculateErgonomicTileOffset and resolveTiledPlane in src/zoom-plane-parser.ts (lines 355–496). Together they implement the signature feature of web-3d-panel-navigation: an author writes data-tile-from-right="center" data-tile-angle="30" on an HTML element, and a tiled panel materialises flush against the right edge of the center panel, folded inward by 30°. No camera math, no manual position bookkeeping, no quaternion sliders. The author describes the layout in physical terms — "from this side, at this fold angle, with this gap and alignment" — and the parser computes an exact world-space position and orientation. Every previous guide (vectors, trig, lerp, rotation matrices, quaternions, frames) feeds into this single function. This is where the math earns its keep.

2. Building intuition

Open a laptop on your desk. The keyboard is the reference panel: it sits flat, wide and rectangular, oriented in some way relative to the world. The screen is the tiled panel: it shares one edge with the keyboard, and folds upward by some angle. Three pieces of geometry describe this perfectly:

  1. The hinge is a single point: the center of the shared edge. For a screen attached to the top of a keyboard of width \(W_R\), the hinge sits at the midpoint of the keyboard's top edge.
  2. The fold is a rotation. The screen rotates about an axis embedded in the reference panel — specifically, an axis parallel to the shared edge. For a top-tiled panel, that axis is the reference's horizontal (right) direction. The fold angle \(\alpha\) measures how far the screen has lifted from the coplanar position.
  3. The center of the tiled panel is recovered by walking from the hinge along the tiled panel's own (post-fold) axis by half its size. If the screen has height \(H_T\), and we just folded it upward, the center sits at the hinge plus \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_T \cdot H_T/2\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_T\) is the screen's up direction after folding.

The crucial moment is step 3. The displacement from hinge to tiled-panel center is along the post-fold axis, not the original axis. That is what guarantees the screen's bottom edge meets the keyboard's top edge perfectly: the hinge is on both panels' edges by construction, and the tiled panel's edge extends outward symmetrically from its center, so half the size in either direction lands on either edge. The hinge is the shared edge.

This is the same observation that makes a real laptop hinge work. The hinge is on the keyboard edge and on the screen edge simultaneously, no matter the fold angle. Open the laptop to 90°, 120°, 180° (flat) — the bottom edge of the screen never separates from the top edge of the keyboard.

3. The pipeline

Symbol conventions for the rest of this guide:

SymbolMeaning
\(\mathbf{c}_R, q_R\)Reference panel center and quaternion
\(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_R, \hat{\mathbf{n}}_R\)Reference right, up, normal (unit vectors in world space)
\(\mathbf{c}_T, q_T\)Tiled panel center and quaternion
\(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_T, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_T, \hat{\mathbf{n}}_T\)Tiled right, up, normal
\(W_R, H_R, W_T, H_T\)Reference and tiled widths and heights (unscaled)
\(s\)Global scale factor (default 0.5)
\(\alpha\)Tile fold angle in radians
\(\mathbf{o} = (o_x, o_y)\)In-frame hinge offset (reference's local right/up)
\(\mathbf{e} = (e_x, e_y, e_z)\)Rotation-offset Euler triple in radians

Step 1 — recover the reference frame

The reference panel arrives with its position and rotation already resolved (either explicitly authored or recursively resolved from its reference). We rebuild its quaternion from its Euler angles in 'XYZ' order, then rotate the standard basis to get its world-space right and up axes (guide 06):

$$ q_R = \operatorname{fromEuler}((r_x^R, r_y^R, r_z^R), \text{XYZ}) $$ $$ \hat{\mathbf{r}}_R = R_{q_R}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}}_R = R_{q_R}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}} $$ 
const referenceQuaternion = new THREE.Quaternion().setFromEuler(
  new THREE.Euler(reference.rotation[0], reference.rotation[1], reference.rotation[2], 'XYZ')
);
const referenceRight = new THREE.Vector3(1, 0, 0).applyQuaternion(referenceQuaternion);
const referenceUp    = new THREE.Vector3(0, 1, 0).applyQuaternion(referenceQuaternion);

Step 2 — locate the hinge

The hinge starts at the center of the reference panel's shared edge. Which edge depends on which side the tiled panel attaches to. In all cases we walk from \(\mathbf{c}_R\) along \(\pm\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R\) or \(\pm\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R\) by half the relevant dimension (scaled):

$$ \mathbf{h}_0 = \begin{cases} \mathbf{c}_R + \hat{\mathbf{r}}_R \cdot s W_R / 2 & \text{(right)} \\ \mathbf{c}_R - \hat{\mathbf{r}}_R \cdot s W_R / 2 & \text{(left)} \\ \mathbf{c}_R + \hat{\mathbf{u}}_R \cdot s H_R / 2 & \text{(top)} \\ \mathbf{c}_R - \hat{\mathbf{u}}_R \cdot s H_R / 2 & \text{(bottom)} \end{cases} $$

Then we add the in-frame ergonomic offset (computed in step 4 below):

$$ \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{h}_0 + o_x \hat{\mathbf{r}}_R + o_y \hat{\mathbf{u}}_R $$

The offset is added in the reference's local right/up axes — that is what makes data-tile-offset="40, -20" "shift the hinge 40 right and 20 down relative to the reference", regardless of how the reference itself is rotated.

Step 3 — the fold quaternion

The fold is an axis-angle rotation. The project picks a world axis: \((0, 1, 0)\) for left/right tiling and \((1, 0, 0)\) for top/bottom tiling. Sign convention by side:

$$ q_{\text{fold}} = \begin{cases} \operatorname{axisAngle}((0,1,0), -\alpha) & \text{(right)} \\ \operatorname{axisAngle}((0,1,0), +\alpha) & \text{(left)} \\ \operatorname{axisAngle}((1,0,0), +\alpha) & \text{(top)} \\ \operatorname{axisAngle}((1,0,0), -\alpha) & \text{(bottom)} \end{cases} $$

Why these signs? Picture a top-down view of the world (looking down the y-axis). The reference panel is a horizontal line through the origin, with normal pointing toward +z. A right-tiled panel folds inward — toward +z, the side we are facing. A right-handed rotation about \(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) by a positive angle would swing points on the +x side away from us (toward -z). To fold toward +z we need a negative angle around \(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) — equivalently, a positive angle about \(-\hat{\mathbf{y}}\). Hence \(q_{\text{fold,right}} = \operatorname{axisAngle}((0,1,0), -\alpha)\).

For a left-tiled panel, the panel sits on the -x side. A positive rotation about \(+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) swings points on -x toward +z (toward us). So left uses \(+\alpha\). This is opposite-handedness from the right side because the panel sits on opposite sides of the hinge axis. Top and bottom mirror the same logic, but with the fold axis now \(+\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) (looking from the side).

The fold axis is the world \((0,1,0)\) or \((1,0,0)\), not the reference's local \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R\) or \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R\). That distinction matters: it is what lets us write the composition \(q_R \cdot q_{\text{offset}} \cdot q_{\text{fold}}\) cleanly. The fold is expressed in "reference-default" coordinates, and \(q_R\) on the outer left rotates the whole result into the reference's actual orientation. See step 4.

Step 4 — compose the tiled-panel quaternion

$$ q_T = q_R \cdot q_{\text{offset}} \cdot q_{\text{fold}}, \qquad q_{\text{offset}} = \operatorname{fromEuler}(\mathbf{e}, \text{XYZ}) $$

Quaternion multiplication is non-commutative (guide 05): order matters. To read what each factor does, apply \(q_T\) to a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) and unwrap right-to-left:

  1. \(q_{\text{fold}}\,\mathbf{v}\) — rotate \(\mathbf{v}\) about the world fold axis by \(\pm\alpha\). At this point we are pretending the reference is at world-default orientation.
  2. \(q_{\text{offset}}\) — apply the user's extra reference-relative tilt. Because we have not yet rotated into the reference's frame, this rotation acts on the "reference-default" vector — which is exactly what "reference-relative" means.
  3. \(q_R\) — rotate the whole result into the reference's actual world orientation.

Equivalent reading: the fold happens as if the reference were at world-default orientation, then the offset perturbs it in that same reference frame, then \(q_R\) carries the entire assembly into the world. This is why the fold axis can be \((0,1,0)\) — it is the reference's pre-rotation up axis. 

const offsetQuaternion = new THREE.Quaternion().setFromEuler(
  new THREE.Euler(
    layout.rotationOffset[0],
    layout.rotationOffset[1],
    layout.rotationOffset[2],
    'XYZ'
  )
);
const quaternion = referenceQuaternion.clone()
  .multiply(offsetQuaternion)
  .multiply(relativeQuaternion);

Step 5 — recover the tiled frame

$$ \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T = R_{q_T}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{u}}_T = R_{q_T}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}} $$ 
const newRight = new THREE.Vector3(1, 0, 0).applyQuaternion(quaternion);
const newUp    = new THREE.Vector3(0, 1, 0).applyQuaternion(quaternion);

Step 6 — locate the tiled center from the hinge

Walk from the hinge along the tiled panel's (post-fold) axis by half its size:

$$ \mathbf{c}_T = \begin{cases} \mathbf{h} + \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot s W_T / 2 & \text{(right)} \\ \mathbf{h} - \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot s W_T / 2 & \text{(left)} \\ \mathbf{h} + \hat{\mathbf{u}}_T \cdot s H_T / 2 & \text{(top)} \\ \mathbf{h} - \hat{\mathbf{u}}_T \cdot s H_T / 2 & \text{(bottom)} \end{cases} $$

This is the moment that "feels right". The hinge \(\mathbf{h}\) lies on the reference panel's shared edge by construction. The displacement from \(\mathbf{h}\) to \(\mathbf{c}_T\) is along the tiled panel's own axis, by half its size — so the tiled panel's near edge passes through \(\mathbf{h}\). Both panels' shared edges meet at \(\mathbf{h}\), regardless of \(\alpha\). The tests in zoom-plane-parser.test.ts check exactly this property: 

// from zoom-plane-parser.test.ts:205-208
expectVectorClose(
  edgeCenter(rightConfig, 'left', scale),
  edgeCenter(centerConfig, 'right', scale)
);

4. Ergonomic offset layers

calculateErgonomicTileOffset builds the in-frame hinge offset \((o_x, o_y)\) from four layers, added in this order: gap → align → align-offset → manual.

Layer 1 — gap

Pushes the tiled panel away from the reference edge along the perpendicular-to-the-shared-edge direction in-frame. For right/left tiling the shared edge runs vertically, so gap acts along \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R\); for top/bottom tiling the shared edge runs horizontally, so gap acts along \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R\). The sign matches the side, so a positive gap always pushes the tiled panel away:

SideGap contribution
right\(o_x \mathrel{+}= +\text{gap}\)
left\(o_x \mathrel{+}= -\text{gap}\)
top\(o_y \mathrel{+}= +\text{gap}\)
bottom\(o_y \mathrel{+}= -\text{gap}\)

Layer 2 — align

Compensates for size differences. For right/left tiling, alignment is vertical (top / center / bottom). The reference and tiled panels may have different heights; aligning to "top" shifts the tiled panel up so its top edge matches the reference's top edge. The shift formula is just "shift so the named edges coincide".

If the reference height is \(H_R\) and the tiled height is \(H_T\), define \(\Delta H = (H_R - H_T) \cdot s\). Center alignment leaves the centers of the two panels' shared-edge projections aligned (zero shift). Top alignment requires the tiled center to sit \(\Delta H / 2\) higher than the reference center (so the top edges, each \(\pm H/2\) from their respective centers, line up). Bottom is the mirror.

$$ \text{align-shift}_y = \begin{cases} +\Delta H / 2 & \text{align = top} \\ 0 & \text{align = center} \\ -\Delta H / 2 & \text{align = bottom} \end{cases} $$

For top/bottom tiling the same logic plays out with widths along \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R\):

$$ \Delta W = (W_R - W_T) \cdot s, \qquad \text{align-shift}_x = \begin{cases} -\Delta W / 2 & \text{align = left} \\ 0 & \text{align = center} \\ +\Delta W / 2 & \text{align = right} \end{cases} $$

Layer 3 — align offset

A user-supplied scalar nudge along the same axis as the alignment. For right/left tiling it adds to \(o_y\); for top/bottom tiling it adds to \(o_x\). It rides on top of the align shift — so you can say "align top, then nudge 12 units further up".

Layer 4 — manual offset (data-tile-offset)

The literal user-supplied 2D offset \((m_x, m_y)\), added directly to \((o_x, o_y)\) with no further interpretation. This is the lowest-level escape hatch.

The full formula:

$$ (o_x, o_y) = \underbrace{(\text{gap}_x, \text{gap}_y)}_{\text{layer 1}} + \underbrace{(\text{align}_x, \text{align}_y)}_{\text{layer 2}} + \underbrace{(\text{align-offset}_x, \text{align-offset}_y)}_{\text{layer 3}} + \underbrace{(m_x, m_y)}_{\text{layer 4}} $$

And the offset stack, the entirety of calculateErgonomicTileOffset (lines 355–404):

function calculateErgonomicTileOffset(
  layout: TiledPlaneDefinition,
  definition: PlaneDefinition,
  reference: ZoomPlaneConfig,
  scale: number
): [number, number] {
  let gapX = 0;
  let gapY = 0;
  let alignX = 0;
  let alignY = 0;

  if (layout.side === 'right' || layout.side === 'left') {
    // ... validation ...
    gapX = layout.side === 'right' ? layout.gap : -layout.gap;

    const heightDelta = (reference.height - definition.height) * scale;
    if (layout.align === 'top') {
      alignY = heightDelta / 2;
    } else if (layout.align === 'bottom') {
      alignY = -heightDelta / 2;
    }
    alignY += layout.alignOffset;
  } else {
    // ... validation ...
    gapY = layout.side === 'top' ? layout.gap : -layout.gap;

    const widthDelta = (reference.width - definition.width) * scale;
    if (layout.align === 'left') {
      alignX = -widthDelta / 2;
    } else if (layout.align === 'right') {
      alignX = widthDelta / 2;
    }
    alignX += layout.alignOffset;
  }

  return [
    gapX + alignX + layout.offset[0],
    gapY + alignY + layout.offset[1],
  ];
}

7. Worked examples

Example A — flat reference, right tile, 30°

Reference: \(\mathbf{c}_R = (0,0,0)\), rotation \((0,0,0)\), \(W_R = 1600\), \(H_R = 900\), \(s = 1\). Tiled: \(W_T = 1280\), \(H_T = 720\), tile-from-right="center", tile-angle="30", all other tile attributes default (gap = 0, align = "center", offset = 0,0, rotation-offset = 0,0,0).

  1. \(q_R = (0,0,0,1)\), \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R = (1,0,0)\), \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R = (0,1,0)\).
  2. Hinge base: \(\mathbf{h}_0 = (0,0,0) + (1,0,0) \cdot 1600/2 = (800, 0, 0)\). Ergonomic offset is \((0,0)\) (defaults). So \(\mathbf{h} = (800, 0, 0)\).
  3. Fold: \(q_{\text{fold}} = \operatorname{axisAngle}((0,1,0), -30°)\). In quaternion form, \(q_{\text{fold}} = (0, \sin(-15°), 0, \cos(-15°)) \approx (0, -0.2588, 0, 0.9659)\).
  4. \(q_{\text{offset}} = (0,0,0,1)\), so \(q_T = q_R \cdot q_{\text{offset}} \cdot q_{\text{fold}} = q_{\text{fold}}\).
  5. \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_T = R_{q_T}(1,0,0) = (\cos(-30°), 0, -\sin(-30°)) = (\cos 30°, 0, \sin 30°) \approx (0.8660, 0, 0.5)\).
  6. \(\mathbf{c}_T = \mathbf{h} + \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot 1280/2 = (800, 0, 0) + (0.8660, 0, 0.5) \cdot 640\) \(\;= (800 + 554.26, 0, 320) \approx (1354.26, 0, 320)\).

The tiled panel sits 554 units further right and 320 units in front of (positive z) the keyboard's right edge — exactly what a 30° fold predicts.

Example B — flat reference, top tile, 90°

Reference as in A. Tiled: \(W_T = 1600\), \(H_T = 900\), tile-from-top="center", tile-angle="90", defaults elsewhere.

  1. Hinge: \(\mathbf{h} = (0,0,0) + (0,1,0) \cdot 900/2 = (0, 450, 0)\).
  2. Fold: \(q_{\text{fold}} = \operatorname{axisAngle}((1,0,0), +90°)\). Then \(q_T = q_{\text{fold}}\).
  3. \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_T = R_{q_T}(0,1,0) = (0, \cos 90°, \sin 90°) = (0, 0, 1)\).
  4. \(\mathbf{c}_T = (0, 450, 0) + (0, 0, 1) \cdot 900/2 = (0, 450, 450)\).

A 90° fold makes the tiled panel face forward (+z), with its center 450 units forward of the hinge — half its height, as expected.

Example C — gap and alignment

Reference: \(W_R = 1000\), \(H_R = 500\), at origin, no rotation, \(s = 0.5\). Tiled: \(W_T = 800\), \(H_T = 300\), tile-from-right, tile-angle="0" (no fold), tile-gap="40", tile-align="top", defaults elsewhere.

  1. \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R = (1,0,0)\), \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R = (0,1,0)\).
  2. Base hinge: \(\mathbf{h}_0 = (0,0,0) + (1,0,0) \cdot 1000 \cdot 0.5 / 2 = (250, 0, 0)\).
  3. Ergonomic offset:
    • gap: side=right, so \(o_x = +40\), \(o_y = 0\).
    • align: \(\Delta H = (500 - 300) \cdot 0.5 = 100\). align = top, so \(o_y\mathrel{+}= 100/2 = 50\).
    • align-offset, manual offset: 0.
    Total: \((o_x, o_y) = (40, 50)\).
  4. \(\mathbf{h} = (250, 0, 0) + 40 \cdot (1,0,0) + 50 \cdot (0,1,0) = (290, 50, 0)\).
  5. Fold angle is 0, so \(q_T = (0,0,0,1)\), \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_T = (1,0,0)\).
  6. \(\mathbf{c}_T = (290, 50, 0) + (1,0,0) \cdot 800 \cdot 0.5 / 2 = (290 + 200, 50, 0) = (490, 50, 0)\).

The tiled panel's center is 50 units up (because the top edges align across a 100-unit height difference, scaled), and 40 units further right than it would be without the gap.

Solution 1

\(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_R = (1,0,0)\). Hinge: \(\mathbf{h}_0 = (0,0,0) - (1,0,0)\cdot 1000/2 = (-500, 0, 0)\). All ergonomic offsets are 0, so \(\mathbf{h} = (-500,0,0)\).

Fold for left side: \(q_{\text{fold}} = \operatorname{axisAngle}((0,1,0), +60°)\). \(q_T = q_{\text{fold}}\). Euler XYZ: \((0°, 60°, 0°)\).

\(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_T = R_{q_T}(1,0,0) = (\cos 60°, 0, -\sin 60°) = (0.5, 0, -0.8660)\).

\(\mathbf{c}_T = \mathbf{h} - \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot 600/2 = (-500, 0, 0) - (0.5, 0, -0.8660)\cdot 300 = (-650, 0, 259.81)\).

Solution 2

\(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R = (0,1,0)\). Hinge: \(\mathbf{h}_0 = (0,0,0) - (0,1,0)\cdot 500/2 = (0, -250, 0)\). All ergonomic offsets are 0, so \(\mathbf{h} = (0,-250,0)\).

Fold for bottom side: \(q_{\text{fold}} = \operatorname{axisAngle}((1,0,0), -45°)\). \(q_T = q_{\text{fold}}\). Euler XYZ: \((-45°, 0°, 0°)\).

\(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_T = R_{q_T}(0,1,0) = (0, \cos(-45°), \sin(-45°)) = (0, 0.7071, -0.7071)\).

\(\mathbf{c}_T = \mathbf{h} - \hat{\mathbf{u}}_T\cdot 800/2 = (0, -250, 0) - (0, 0.7071, -0.7071)\cdot 400 = (0, -532.84, 282.84)\).

Solution 3

The composition reads right-to-left when applied to a vector. The fold acts first, before \(q_R\) carries the result into the reference's world orientation. So at fold time, the vector is still in "reference-default" coordinates — coordinates where the reference's right axis is the world \((1,0,0)\) and its up axis is the world \((0,1,0)\). Choosing the fold axis to be world \((0,1,0)\) for left/right tiling is therefore the same as choosing the reference's local up axis, but expressed in the convenient pre-rotation frame. If we used \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_R\) directly, we would have to right-multiply rather than left-multiply, and the offset rotation would no longer compose cleanly.

Solution 4

At \(\alpha = 180°\), \(q_{\text{fold,right}} = \operatorname{axisAngle}((0,1,0), -180°)\). Apply to \((1,0,0)\) and you get \((-1, 0, 0)\). So \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_T = (-1,0,0)\). Then \(\mathbf{c}_T = \mathbf{h} + (-1,0,0)\cdot sW_T/2 = \mathbf{h} - sW_T/2 \,\hat{\mathbf{x}}\). Visually, the tiled panel has folded all the way around and now lies flush behind the reference panel — its center is on the same side as the reference's left edge. The hinge property still holds: the tiled panel's left edge (now in the +x direction, since the panel is flipped) passes through \(\mathbf{h}\). The two panels are coplanar but back-to-back-rotated by 180° about y.

Solution 5

With \(q_R \cdot q_{\text{fold}} \cdot q_{\text{offset}}\), reading right-to-left: \(q_{\text{offset}}\) acts first on the vector — but at that moment we are in pre-fold, pre-reference coordinates. The "offset" is now applied in world-default coordinates, not in the reference's frame. After folding and then orienting via \(q_R\), the offset's effect is in a different frame than before. Concretely, a positive \(e_y\) (rotation offset around y) used to mean "yaw the tiled panel a bit further around the reference's local up"; in the swapped order, it means "yaw the tiled panel around its own pre-reference axis after folding", which is generally a different physical rotation. The tests in zoom-plane-parser.test.ts:510-545 assert the original order specifically.

Solution 6

For right tiling: the tiled panel's left edge midpoint is \(\mathbf{c}_T - \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot sW_T/2\). But step 6 sets \(\mathbf{c}_T = \mathbf{h} + \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot sW_T/2\). Substituting, the left edge midpoint is \(\mathbf{h} + \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot sW_T/2 - \hat{\mathbf{r}}_T \cdot sW_T/2 = \mathbf{h}\). The cancellation is exact and independent of \(\alpha\). The same argument applies to the other three sides with the obvious sign and axis substitutions.

Solution 7

"In front" means displaced along \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_R\). Step 2: \(\mathbf{h}_0 = \mathbf{c}_R + \hat{\mathbf{n}}_R \cdot d\) for some user-supplied distance \(d\). The fold axis would naturally be \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_R\) — but that produces a rotation in the same plane as the reference, which is degenerate (no fold visible). More useful is no fold at all, or a "tilt" specified differently. Step 6 has no clean analog because the tiled panel is not edge-attached, so the "walk along post-fold axis by half the size" idiom does not apply. This is why the package uses edge-tiling rather than face-stacking — the math is fundamentally cleaner when there is a shared edge.

Solution 8

No other step changes. The pipeline only ever uses \(q_{\text{offset}}\) as a quaternion in the multiplication \(q_R \cdot q_{\text{offset}} \cdot q_{\text{fold}}\). The Euler-from-XYZ construction is purely a convenient input format. A user-supplied quaternion would slot in directly. (In fact, this is one reason the project's API exposes Euler angles publicly but uses quaternions internally — Euler input is more author-friendly, but quaternions are what the math actually needs.)
