Guide 08 — FOV, Frustum, and Camera Framing

Eighth in the math curriculum for web-3d-panel-navigation. Prerequisites: guide 02 (trig: degrees / radians, $\tan$, similar triangles), guide 06 (frames: applying Euler rotations to get a plane's normal), and a glance at guide 07 (the same $\tan(\theta/2)$ that built the projection matrix is about to reappear here as a framing tool).

1. Why this matters

Two core operations in this library both reduce to the same question: how far should the camera sit from a target so that the target fits the viewport?

  • Zooming into a single panel. The detail (perpendicular) state is computed in src/camera-controller.ts, lines 43–86. The user clicks a panel; the camera flies to a position normal to that panel, at exactly the distance that makes the panel fill fillPercentage of the viewport on whichever axis is the binding constraint.
  • Framing the whole scene. The overview state is computed in src/overview-camera.ts, lines 58–156. Either we frame onto a focal plane (Focal Element Mode) and pull back enough to keep every other plane in shot, or we center on the geometric mean of all planes (Balanced Scene Mode) and pull back enough to fit the bounding box plus padding.

Both call sites end up writing the same three lines:

const distanceForHeight = (paddedHeight / 2) / halfFovTan;
const distanceForWidth  = (paddedWidth  / 2) / (halfFovTan * aspectRatio);
const requiredDistance  = Math.max(distanceForHeight, distanceForWidth);

This guide derives that pattern from a right triangle, then walks through the project's framing code line by line.

2. Building intuition

Picture standing at the apex of a pyramid that has been laid on its side, point toward you. The four walls of the pyramid expand outward as they get further from your eye. That is the viewing frustum: the region of 3D space the camera can actually see.

Frustum. A truncated pyramid. The apex is the camera. The cone widens as you move along the optical axis; everything inside it lands somewhere on screen, everything outside is clipped.

The field of view $\theta$ is the full opening angle of that pyramid, measured top-edge-to-bottom-edge through the apex. (Three.js's PerspectiveCamera.fov is the vertical FOV in degrees, which is the convention this project uses.)

Now the key geometric observation: at any distance $d$ in front of the camera, the frustum cuts out a rectangle. The height of that rectangle is some function $h(d)$. By similar triangles — double the distance, double the height — that function is linear:

$$h(d) = (\text{some constant depending on }\theta) \cdot d.$$

That constant is what we are about to derive. Once we have it, the framing problem becomes algebra: given a target height $h_t$ that we want to fill, solve for $d$.

3. The math

3.1 Frustum half-height: $h/2 = d \tan(\theta/2)$

Drop the optical axis horizontally. Place the camera at the apex. Walk a distance $d$ along the axis to where the image plane sits. The top edge of the frustum, at that distance, is some height $h/2$ above the axis.

By symmetry the FOV is split evenly above and below the axis, so the angle between the axis and the top edge is $\theta/2$. We now have a right triangle: opposite side is $h/2$, adjacent side is $d$. The trig from guide 02:

$$\tan\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{h/2}{d} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{h}{2} = d\,\tan\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = 2\,d\,\tan\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right).$$

This is exactly the formula src/projection.ts uses to ask "given the camera is here, how tall is the visible slab at the panel's distance":

// projection.ts:175-177
const fovRadians = (camera.fov * Math.PI) / 180;
const viewportHeightAtPlane = 2 * distance * Math.tan(fovRadians / 2);
const viewportWidthAtPlane = viewportHeightAtPlane * (viewportWidth / viewportHeight);
Forward-reference to guide 07. The same factor $\tan(\theta/2)$ also appears inside the perspective projection matrix, where it scales NDC coordinates. Here we are not projecting — we are using the formula in reverse, as a framing tool, to choose where the camera should sit. Same right triangle, opposite direction.

3.2 Inverting for camera distance

Framing is the inverse problem. We know the target height $h_t$ we want filled and the FOV; solve for $d$:

$$d \;=\; \frac{h_t / 2}{\tan(\theta/2)}.$$

Bigger plane $\Rightarrow$ further away. Wider FOV $\Rightarrow$ closer (because $\tan(\theta/2)$ grows).

3.3 Aspect ratio and dual-axis fitting

So far we only constrained the vertical axis. But monitors are not square. Define

$$\text{aspect} = \frac{\text{viewport width}}{\text{viewport height}}.$$

At distance $d$, the visible width is

$$w(d) \;=\; h(d)\cdot \text{aspect} \;=\; 2\,d\,\tan(\theta/2)\cdot \text{aspect}.$$

To frame a target rectangle of $W_t \times H_t$, both axes must clear:

$$h(d) \ge H_t \;\;\text{and}\;\; w(d) \ge W_t.$$

Solve each independently:

$$d_h \;=\; \frac{H_t/2}{\tan(\theta/2)}, \qquad d_w \;=\; \frac{W_t/2}{\tan(\theta/2)\cdot \text{aspect}}.$$

The binding constraint is whichever distance is larger:

$$d \;=\; \max(d_h,\, d_w).$$

Why max, not min? Because we have to satisfy both inequalities. The smaller distance would only satisfy the easier one. The bigger distance is the one that gets both of the plane's edges inside the frustum.

3.4 Fill percentage as denominator scaling

"Fill 80% of the viewport on the binding axis" means: the plane should occupy 80% of the visible slab at distance $d$, not 100%. Look at the height equation again:

$$h(d) \;=\; 2 d \tan(\theta/2).$$

If the plane's height $H_t$ should be only $f$ of the visible height, then the visible height at the chosen $d$ must equal $H_t / f$:

$$\frac{H_t}{f} \;=\; 2 d\,\tan(\theta/2) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d \;=\; \frac{H_t/2}{\tan(\theta/2)\cdot f}.$$

So the fill factor lands in the denominator. That can feel backwards at first — surely a bigger fraction means we are zooming in, so $d$ should grow? No: a bigger fraction (closer to 1) means the panel fills more of the screen, which means we are closer, which means $d$ is smaller. And that is what dividing by a bigger $f$ does.

Numerically, with $f = 0.8$ vs $f = 0.5$, the same plane needs $d / 0.8 = 1.25\,d_0$ vs $d / 0.5 = 2\,d_0$. Lower fill $\Rightarrow$ farther camera $\Rightarrow$ panel looks smaller in the viewport.

3.5 Padding as fractional expansion

The overview camera does not use fillPercentage. Instead, it expands the framing box by a fraction $p$ before solving:

$$W' = W\,(1 + p), \qquad H' = H\,(1 + p).$$

Default $p = 0.15$, i.e. 15% extra room on each axis. This shows up in DEFAULT_CONFIG.overviewPadding in src/types.ts. The two formulations are interchangeable:

$$\text{fill}\ f \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{padding}\ p = \frac{1}{f} - 1.$$

So $f = 0.8 \Leftrightarrow p \approx 0.25$ (the detail framing wants a tighter feel than the overview's softer 15%).

3.6 Depth back-offset

The overview frustum is a 3D pyramid expanding along $-z$ from the camera. If some panels sit behind the focal plane (further from the camera), they are deeper in $z$ than the focal plane's center. The framing distance we just computed assumes the target lives in a single plane at distance $d$. To keep deeper planes in shot, the camera must back up by their additional depth.

The library computes this as the maximum $z$ of the bounding box relative to the focal plane:

// overview-camera.ts:89-94
const maxDepthBehindCenter = Math.max(
  0,
  boundingBox.maxZ - centerPlane.position[2]
);
requiredDistance += maxDepthBehindCenter;

The Math.max(0, ...) guards against the case where every other plane is in front of the focal plane (negative depth difference); in that case there is nothing to back up for. Otherwise, the back-offset is added directly to requiredDistance.

Coordinate convention. Three.js cameras look down the $-z$ axis of their local frame. "In front of the camera" is therefore $-z$ in camera space; "deeper into the scene" along the camera's optical axis is what we are measuring. The world-space boundingBox.maxZ is the back wall of the AABB (covered properly in guide 09).

4. In this codebase

4.1 Perpendicular detail state — camera-controller.ts:51-57

// camera-controller.ts:51-57
const fovRadians = (targetFov * Math.PI) / 180;
const halfFovTan = Math.tan(fovRadians / 2);
const fill = this.config.fillPercentage;

const distanceForHeight = (actualHeight / 2) / (halfFovTan * fill);
const distanceForWidth = (actualWidth / 2) / (halfFovTan * fill * viewportAspect);

Map line by line to section 3:

  • distanceForWidth is $d_w$, also dividing by viewportAspect — section 3.3.
  • Math.max picks the binding axis — section 3.3.

6. Worked examples

All examples use the project defaults from DEFAULT_CONFIG in src/types.ts: detailFov = 50°, fillPercentage = 0.8, overviewPadding = 0.15. Compute $\tan(25°) \approx 0.4663$.

Example 1 — Detail zoom on a 1600 × 900 panel, 1920 × 1080 viewport

Aspect $= 1920/1080 = 1.7778$. Plane already at scale=1 for this example.

$$d_h \;=\; \frac{900/2}{\tan(25°)\cdot 0.8} \;=\; \frac{450}{0.4663\cdot 0.8} \;=\; \frac{450}{0.3730} \;\approx\; 1206.4.$$

$$d_w \;=\; \frac{1600/2}{\tan(25°)\cdot 0.8\cdot 1.7778} \;=\; \frac{800}{0.6630} \;\approx\; 1206.6.$$

$$d \;=\; \max(1206.4,\, 1206.6) \;\approx\; 1206.6.$$

The width is binding — barely. That makes sense: 1600/900 = 1.778 is essentially the same as the viewport's 1920/1080 = 1.778, so the plane just exactly matches the viewport's aspect; both axes hit the fill boundary almost simultaneously. Try the playground with plane width=1600, plane height=900, aspect=1.78, fill=0.8 — both readouts collapse onto the same value.

Example 2 — Tall panel in a wide viewport

Same FOV, fill, viewport aspect 1.78. Plane is now 600 × 1400 (portrait).

$$d_h \;=\; \frac{700}{0.3730} \;\approx\; 1876.7. \qquad d_w \;=\; \frac{300}{0.6630} \;\approx\; 452.5.$$

$$d = \max(1876.7,\, 452.5) = 1876.7,\;\; \text{binding = height}.$$

The height drives everything; the width has tons of slack. The panel will appear vertically centered with huge horizontal margins, just as you would expect for a portrait poster on a widescreen monitor.

Example 3 — Overview with depth back-offset

Three planes whose bounding box is $W = 4000$, $H = 2000$, with a focal plane at $z = 0$ and the deepest plane at $z = -300$. Viewport aspect 1.78, padding 0.15.

$W' = 4000\cdot 1.15 = 4600$. $H' = 2000\cdot 1.15 = 2300$.

$$d_h = \frac{1150}{0.4663} \approx 2466.4. \qquad d_w = \frac{2300}{0.4663\cdot 1.7778} \approx 2774.3.$$

$d_{\text{frame}} = \max(\ldots) \approx 2774.3$. Width binding.

Now the depth term. boundingBox.maxZ is the back wall of the AABB. If the focal plane sits at $z = 0$ and the deepest plane is at $z = -300$, then in this project's convention what sits "behind the focal plane" along the camera's view axis is whichever side the camera is not on — but the literal code computes boundingBox.maxZ - centerPlane.position[2]. If that difference is positive (the bounding box's positive-$z$ wall is past the center plane), back-offset is added. If everything is on the camera side, the Math.max(0, ...) clamps it to zero.

So if maxZ $= 200$ (some plane sits at $z = 200$, between camera and focal plane in this project's typical layout) the offset is $200$, and final $d = 2774.3 + 200 = 2974.3$. Otherwise no offset.

  1. (routine) A panel is 800 × 600. FOV is 60°, fill is 0.8, viewport aspect is 1.5. Compute $d_h$, $d_w$, $d$, and the binding axis.
  2. (routine) Same panel and viewport, FOV is now 30°. Without computing, predict whether $d$ goes up or down. Then compute it and check.
  3. (routine) Inverse: at FOV 50°, distance $d = 1500$, what is the visible height at that distance? What about visible width if aspect = 2.0?
  4. (conceptual) The dual-axis code uses Math.max(distanceForHeight, distanceForWidth). What would happen if it used Math.min instead? Describe what the user would see.
  5. (conceptual) The fill factor is in the denominator: (actualHeight / 2) / (halfFovTan * fill). Show algebraically that this is equivalent to first dividing the visible height target by fill, then framing tightly.
  6. (project-applied) If a developer changes DEFAULT_CONFIG.fillPercentage from 0.8 to 0.5, what happens to the camera distance for a fixed panel? Does the panel look bigger or smaller? Compute the ratio.
  7. (project-applied) A user has set up a portrait panel (600 × 1400) and is testing on a phone in landscape (aspect 1.78) and in portrait (aspect 0.56). Compute $d$ in both cases. Which axis binds in each? Is there a viewport orientation where the same panel uses the smaller distance?

9. Appendix: solutions

Solutions to exercises 1–9

1. $\tan(30°) \approx 0.5774$. $d_h = (300)/(0.5774\cdot 0.8) \approx 649.6$. $d_w = (400)/(0.5774\cdot 0.8\cdot 1.5) \approx 577.4$. $d = 649.6$, height binds. Plane is taller than the 4:3-ish frustum at that distance, so height drives.

2. Smaller FOV $\Rightarrow$ smaller $\tan(\theta/2)$ $\Rightarrow$ bigger $d$ (camera further). $\tan(15°) \approx 0.2679$. $d_h = 300/(0.2679\cdot 0.8) \approx 1399.8$. $d_w = 400/(0.2679\cdot 0.8\cdot 1.5) \approx 1244.3$. Distance roughly doubles. Same binding axis.

3. $h(d) = 2\cdot 1500\cdot \tan(25°) \approx 1399$. $w(d) \approx 1399 \cdot 2.0 \approx 2798$.

4. min would pick whichever axis is easier to satisfy. The other (harder) axis would still overrun the frustum. Visually: one axis of the panel is cropped — the user sees a panel that exits the top/bottom or left/right of the screen. max guarantees both axes fit.

5. Start from the visible-height equation $h(d) = 2 d\tan(\theta/2)$. We want the panel of height $H_t$ to occupy fraction $f$ of $h(d)$, i.e. $h(d) = H_t/f$. Solve: $d = (H_t/f)/(2\tan(\theta/2)) = (H_t/2)/(\tan(\theta/2)\cdot f)$. Identical to the project's expression, with $f$ in the denominator. The "first divide $H_t$ by $f$ then frame tightly" reading and the "scale the denominator" reading are the same algebra.

6. $d \propto 1/f$. Going from $f = 0.8$ to $f = 0.5$ multiplies the distance by $0.8/0.5 = 1.6$. The panel looks smaller on screen because the camera is further away while the plane stays the same size.

7. Plane 600 × 1400, FOV 50°, fill 0.8. Landscape (aspect 1.78): $d_h = 700/(0.4663\cdot 0.8) \approx 1876.7$. $d_w = 300/(0.4663\cdot 0.8\cdot 1.78) \approx 451.6$. $d = 1876.7$, height binds. Portrait (aspect 0.56): $d_h$ unchanged $\approx 1876.7$. $d_w = 300/(0.4663\cdot 0.8\cdot 0.56) \approx 1435.6$. $d = 1876.7$, height still binds — but barely. There is no orientation in this case that gets you closer than $d_h$ since the plane's height is the dominant axis. To shrink $d$ you'd need to either shrink the plane or widen the FOV.

8. Detail framing uses $d = (H_t/2)/(\tan(\theta/2)\cdot f)$. Padding framing uses $d = (H_t(1+p)/2)/\tan(\theta/2)$. Setting these equal and solving: $1/f = 1+p \Rightarrow p = 1/f - 1$. For $f=0.8$, $p = 0.25$. With $H_t=900$, $\theta=50°$, aspect=1.78: detail gives $d_h = 450/(0.4663\cdot 0.8) \approx 1206.4$; padding-form gives $d_h = (900\cdot 1.25/2)/0.4663 = 562.5/0.4663 \approx 1206.4$. Equal.

9. The world-space AABB of a rotated plane includes the plane's tilted footprint projected onto the world axes. A 1000 × 1000 panel rotated 45° about $y$ has a world-space x-extent of $\sqrt{2}\cdot 1000 \approx 1414$, even though no point of the panel is actually 1414 wide along the camera's screen-x axis when viewed perpendicular to it. The current code frames the AABB onto the world's xy-plane, which can demand $d$ values that are larger than necessary. Guide 09's fix is to project each plane's corners into the camera's view-frame and AABB those, rather than AABBing in world space.
