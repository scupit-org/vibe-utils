Guide 15: Uniform Sphere Sampling

Curriculum entry 15 of the web-3d-panel-navigation math notes.

1. Why this matters

Open src/skybox/starfield-skybox.ts and look at lines 64-71. That is the entire engine that decides where every star in the project's starfield skybox lives: 

function samplePointOnUnitSphere(out: THREE.Vector3): void {
  const u = Math.random();
  const v = Math.random();
  const z = 2 * u - 1;
  const phi = 2 * Math.PI * v;
  const r = Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, 1 - z * z));
  out.set(r * Math.cos(phi), r * Math.sin(phi), z);
}

A few lines below (lines 80-90), buildStarGeometry calls this function once per star and scales the result by STAR_RADIUS = 0.95. Eight lines of arithmetic produce 2,500 points that look uniformly scattered across a sphere — no clusters, no thin spots, nothing the eye notices.

Here is the trap: putting points uniformly on a sphere seems like a trivial problem. There are at least two intuitive recipes you can write down in thirty seconds, and both of them produce visibly biased distributions. The naive approaches:

  1. Sample $(x, y, z)$ uniformly in the cube $[-1, 1]^3$, then normalize to length 1.
  2. Sample the polar angle $\theta$ uniformly in $[0, \pi]$ and the azimuth $\phi$ uniformly in $[0, 2\pi)$, then convert to Cartesian.

Recipe (1) clusters stars along the eight cube-corner directions. Recipe (2) piles stars into dense caps at the north and south poles. If we used either, the starfield would have a "directionality" you'd notice the first time you spun the camera. The project's recipe — eight lines, no rejection, no trigonometry beyond cos and sin — gives a truly uniform sphere. This guide is about why.

2. Building intuition

Before any math, three pictures. Each shows a sphere covered with samples from a different recipe. (You can reproduce all three live in the playground below.)

Picture A: cube-then-normalize, no rejection

Start with $(x, y, z)$ each drawn uniformly from $[-1, 1]$. Then divide by $\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$ to push each point onto the unit sphere. It looks plausible — you are using all the points, each ending up at length 1 — but the cube has corners that stick farther from the origin than its faces. A random point near a corner gets normalized into the same direction as many other corner-region points. Visually, you see eight bright clumps pointing toward $(\pm 1, \pm 1, \pm 1)/\sqrt 3$, plus thinner regions toward $(\pm 1, 0, 0)$, $(0, \pm 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, \pm 1)$.

A quick numerical sanity check: bucket the resulting unit vectors by which of the 26 "compass directions" they are closest to (axes, face-diagonals, corner-diagonals). The corners get roughly $\sqrt 3 \approx 1.73$ times the density they should.

Picture B: uniform $(\theta, \phi)$

Now try the textbook spherical-to-Cartesian conversion with both angles drawn uniformly: $\theta \sim \mathcal{U}[0, \pi]$, $\phi \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 2\pi)$, then $(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$. Now you see the opposite failure mode: a tight crown of stars at the north pole, another at the south, and the equator looking sparse. The reason is that an angular "slice" of width $d\theta$ near a pole wraps a tiny circle, while the same slice at the equator wraps a great circle. Equal angular slices have unequal areas.

Picture C: the project's recipe

The third picture is the boring one — and that is exactly the point. No clumps, no poles, no axis preference. Stars look isotropic. The widget below will make this obvious: switch between the strategies and the difference jumps out instantly.

3. The math

3.1 What "uniform on the sphere" means

Let $S^2$ denote the unit sphere — the surface only, not the interior ball. A probability density $f(\mathbf p)$ on $S^2$ is uniform if it is constant per unit surface area. The total surface area of the unit sphere is $4\pi$, so the uniform density is

\[ f(\mathbf p) \;=\; \frac{1}{4\pi}, \qquad \mathbf p \in S^2. \]

"Uniform" here is not "uniform in some parameterization" — it is uniform with respect to area on the sphere itself. Both naive recipes get this wrong by sampling uniformly in the wrong measure.

3.2 The surface element

Parameterize a point on the unit sphere by polar angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ (measured from the north pole, $z = \cos\theta$) and azimuth $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$:

\[ \mathbf p(\theta, \phi) \;=\; \big(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\; \sin\theta\sin\phi,\; \cos\theta\big). \]

A small angular rectangle $d\theta \times d\phi$ does not map to a fixed amount of surface area. The surface element is

\[ dA \;=\; \sin\theta \; d\theta \; d\phi. \]

You can read the $\sin\theta$ factor off the geometry: a horizontal "ring" at polar angle $\theta$ is a circle of radius $\sin\theta$ (because the ring sits distance $\sin\theta$ from the $z$-axis). Its circumference is $2\pi\sin\theta$, so the band between $\theta$ and $\theta + d\theta$ has area $2\pi\sin\theta \cdot d\theta$. Splitting the band into azimuthal slices of width $d\phi$ gives $dA = \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\phi$. At the equator $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\sin\theta = 1$; at a pole $\sin\theta = 0$.

3.3 Why the naive uniform-$(\theta, \phi)$ fails

If we draw $\theta$ uniformly on $[0, \pi]$ and $\phi$ uniformly on $[0, 2\pi)$, the joint density in $(\theta, \phi)$ space is constant: $f_{\theta,\phi}(\theta,\phi) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}$. To compute the induced density on the sphere we have to divide by the area Jacobian $\sin\theta$ (since one unit of $(\theta, \phi)$-space covers $\sin\theta$ units of sphere area). The resulting density on $S^2$ is

\[ f_{S^2}(\theta, \phi) \;=\; \frac{f_{\theta,\phi}(\theta,\phi)}{\sin\theta} \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi^2 \sin\theta}. \]

Near a pole, $\sin\theta \to 0$, so $f_{S^2} \to \infty$. That is the mathematical fingerprint of the visual pole-clustering you saw in Picture B.

3.4 The fix: change variables to $z = \cos\theta$

The $\sin\theta$ factor is annoying because it depends on $\theta$. Can we absorb it into a clean uniform sample? Yes — by changing the polar variable from $\theta$ to $z = \cos\theta$.

Compute the differential: $z = \cos\theta$, so $dz = -\sin\theta \, d\theta$, and $|dz| = \sin\theta \, d\theta$. Substituting into the surface element:

\[ dA \;=\; \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\phi \;=\; |dz| \, d\phi \;=\; dz \, d\phi \quad (\text{after fixing the sign with the } z \text{ range}). \]

This is the key result. In the variables $(z, \phi)$, the surface element is just $dz \, d\phi$. Equal rectangles in $(z, \phi)$-space cover equal amounts of sphere area. As $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$, $z$ runs from $+1$ to $-1$, so the parameter ranges are $z \in [-1, 1]$ and $\phi \in [0, 2\pi)$.

Sampling uniformly in $(z, \phi)$-space is therefore the same as sampling uniformly in surface area. Concretely: draw

\[ z \sim \mathcal{U}[-1, 1], \qquad \phi \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 2\pi), \]

then recover the Cartesian coordinates. Since $\sin\theta = +\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\theta} = \sqrt{1 - z^2}$ (positive because $\theta \in [0, \pi]$), the point is

\[ \mathbf p \;=\; \Big(\sqrt{1 - z^2}\,\cos\phi, \;\;\sqrt{1 - z^2}\,\sin\phi, \;\;z\Big). \]

3.5 The project's recipe, line by line

The implementation in starfield-skybox.ts turns this into eight lines of code:

const u = Math.random();              // u ~ U[0, 1)
const v = Math.random();              // v ~ U[0, 1)
const z = 2 * u - 1;                  // z = 2u - 1   ~ U[-1, 1)
const phi = 2 * Math.PI * v;          // phi = 2*pi*v ~ U[0, 2*pi)
const r = Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, 1 - z * z));   // r = sqrt(1 - z^2) = sin(theta)
out.set(r * Math.cos(phi), r * Math.sin(phi), z);

Two notes on the code:

  • The shifts and scales. 2 * u - 1 turns $\mathcal{U}[0, 1)$ into $\mathcal{U}[-1, 1)$. 2 * Math.PI * v turns $\mathcal{U}[0, 1)$ into $\mathcal{U}[0, 2\pi)$. Both are inverse-CDF sampling for the uniform distributions on those intervals.
  • The defensive Math.max(0, ...). Algebraically $1 - z^2 \ge 0$ for $z \in [-1, 1]$. But Math.random() occasionally returns values like 0.9999999998, and floating-point arithmetic rounds 1 - z*z below zero by an epsilon. Without the clamp, the subsequent Math.sqrt returns NaN and the star ends up at $(\text{NaN}, \text{NaN}, \pm 1)$, which on a GPU buffer means an invisible or garbage star. Cheap insurance.

3.6 The general principle: inverse-transform sampling

Guide 14 introduced inverse-transform sampling for the warm/cool tint power law: to draw from a distribution with CDF $F$, evaluate $F^{-1}$ at a uniform random number. Here we are using the same idea twice in a row.

For the azimuth: the CDF of $\mathcal{U}[0, 2\pi)$ is $F_\phi(\phi) = \phi / (2\pi)$, so $F_\phi^{-1}(v) = 2\pi v$. That is exactly 2 * Math.PI * v.

For the cosine of the polar angle: the marginal density of $z$ that gives uniform area is $f_z(z) = 1/2$ on $[-1, 1]$. Its CDF is $F_z(z) = (z + 1)/2$, so $F_z^{-1}(u) = 2u - 1$. That is exactly 2 * u - 1.

The unified story: each call to Math.random() is a uniform $\mathcal{U}[0, 1)$ sample, and we apply the inverse CDF of the target distribution to it. The cleverness is in step 3.4 — recognizing that the right coordinate to make uniform is $z = \cos\theta$, not $\theta$ itself.

3.7 Comparison with rejection sampling

There is a third correct recipe, conceptually simpler than ours: cube-then-reject-then-normalize. 

// Correct, but wasteful.
do {
  x = 2 * Math.random() - 1;
  y = 2 * Math.random() - 1;
  z = 2 * Math.random() - 1;
} while (x*x + y*y + z*z > 1 || x*x + y*y + z*z < 1e-12);
const inv = 1 / Math.sqrt(x*x + y*y + z*z);
out.set(x * inv, y * inv, z * inv);

Rejecting points outside the inscribed sphere fixes the corner-clumping problem (because surviving points are uniform inside the ball, and projecting a uniformly-distributed ball point to the surface is unbiased). It is correct, but every point has only a $\frac{4}{3}\pi / 8 = \pi/6 \approx 0.524$ chance of being inside the sphere. So roughly $1 - \pi/6 \approx 47.6\%$ of generated triples are thrown away.

The inverse-transform recipe never throws anything out: every $(u, v)$ pair becomes a star. For a starfield with thousands of points, the savings are not dramatic (rejection finishes in milliseconds anyway), but the rejection-free recipe is also branch-free and trivially vectorizable. That is why production graphics code overwhelmingly uses it.

4. In this codebase

Here is the function in full, with no edits, plus the loop that calls it. From src/skybox/starfield-skybox.ts: 

// Lines 64-71
function samplePointOnUnitSphere(out: THREE.Vector3): void {
  const u = Math.random();
  const v = Math.random();
  const z = 2 * u - 1;
  const phi = 2 * Math.PI * v;
  const r = Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, 1 - z * z));
  out.set(r * Math.cos(phi), r * Math.sin(phi), z);
}

// Lines 73-98 (inside buildStarGeometry)
const positions = new Float32Array(count * 3);
const scratch = new THREE.Vector3();

for (let i = 0; i < count; i++) {
  samplePointOnUnitSphere(scratch);
  positions[i * 3 + 0] = scratch.x * STAR_RADIUS;
  positions[i * 3 + 1] = scratch.y * STAR_RADIUS;
  positions[i * 3 + 2] = scratch.z * STAR_RADIUS;
  // ... per-star phase, brightness, tint also generated here.
}

Three things to notice:

  • The function fills an out-parameter (THREE.Vector3) instead of returning a fresh object. This avoids allocating 2,500 temporary vectors per skybox build — micro-optimization, but standard Three.js style.
  • After sampling, every component is scaled by STAR_RADIUS = 0.95. Stars sit on a sphere of radius $0.95$, just inside the gradient skybox shell. Sampling on the unit sphere first and scaling later is conceptually cleaner than baking the radius into the sampler.
  • The same scratch vector is reused for every star.

There is no rejection loop, no while, no normalization step. The 2,500 stars are fully placed in 2,500 deterministic-cost iterations.

6. Worked examples

  1. Compute one sample. Suppose Math.random() returns $u = 0.7$ and then $v = 0.25$. Apply the recipe.
    • $z = 2(0.7) - 1 = 0.4$.
    • $\phi = 2\pi(0.25) = \pi/2 \approx 1.5708$.
    • $r = \sqrt{1 - 0.4^2} = \sqrt{0.84} \approx 0.9165$.
    • $x = r\cos\phi \approx 0.9165 \cdot 0 = 0$.
    • $y = r\sin\phi \approx 0.9165 \cdot 1 = 0.9165$.
    • $\mathbf p \approx (0, 0.9165, 0.4)$. Sanity: $|\mathbf p|^2 = 0 + 0.84 + 0.16 = 1$. Good.
  2. A near-pole edge case. Suppose Math.random() returns $u = 0.9999999998$, $v = 0.0$.
    • $z = 2u - 1 = 0.9999999996$.
    • $\phi = 0$.
    • $1 - z^2 \approx 8 \times 10^{-10}$ algebraically, but rounded to a Float64 it can come out negative (e.g. $-1.1 \times 10^{-16}$).
    • Without Math.max(0, ...): Math.sqrt(-1.1e-16) is NaN, the star is at $(\text{NaN}, \text{NaN}, z)$, GPU drops or scrambles it.
    • With the clamp: $r = 0$, point is $(0, 0, z) \approx$ north pole, which is correct.
  3. Bucket the $z$-distribution by hand. With $N = 1000$ samples drawn from the project's recipe and 10 buckets of width $0.2$ on $z \in [-1, 1]$, what should the histogram look like? Since $z$ is uniform on $[-1, 1]$, each bucket has probability $0.1$ and expected count $100$. The Poisson standard deviation is $\sqrt{100} = 10$, so a typical run gives bin counts in the range $80\text{–}120$. Compare with the naive uniform-$(\theta, \phi)$ recipe: $z = \cos\theta$ with $\theta$ uniform on $[0, \pi]$, so $z$ has density $\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1-z^2}}$, which goes to infinity at $z = \pm 1$. Bins at the ends of the $z$-axis overflow; the middle bin near $z = 0$ is short. That is the pole-clumping in numbers.

Equirectangular images are how panorama textures get baked: a 2:1 image where pixel column maps to $\phi$ and pixel row maps to $\theta$. It is a different parameterization of the same sphere we sampled here. As you saw in the playground, "uniform in equirectangular pixel space" is exactly the bad uniform-$(\theta, \phi)$ distribution from this guide. 9. Appendix: Solutions

Solutions to exercises
  1. $u = 0.5$, $v = 0.5$. Then $z = 2(0.5) - 1 = 0$, $\phi = 2\pi(0.5) = \pi$, $r = \sqrt{1 - 0} = 1$. So $x = \cos\pi = -1$, $y = \sin\pi = 0$, and $\mathbf p = (-1, 0, 0)$. The point lies on the equator, on the negative $x$-axis.
  2. $u = 0.0$, $v = 0.75$. Then $z = 2(0) - 1 = -1$, so this is the south pole. $\phi = 2\pi(0.75) = 3\pi/2$, but it does not matter — at the pole, $r = \sqrt{1 - 1} = 0$, so $x = y = 0$ regardless of $\phi$. Final point: $(0, 0, -1)$.
  3. The surface element $dA = \sin\theta \, d\theta \, d\phi$ contains a $\sin\theta$ factor that varies with $\theta$. If you make $\theta$ uniform, then $\theta$-density is constant, but the actual area-density on the sphere is $\frac{1}{\sin\theta}$ times that — infinite at the poles, smaller at the equator. Switching to $z = \cos\theta$ absorbs the $\sin\theta$ into $|dz| = \sin\theta \, d\theta$, leaving $dA = dz \, d\phi$. Now uniform-in-$z$ is uniform-in-area.
  4. The cube $[-1, 1]^3$ has volume $2^3 = 8$. The unit ball has volume $\frac{4}{3}\pi$. The probability that a uniform cube point falls inside the ball is $\frac{4\pi/3}{8} = \frac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.5236$, so the rejection rate is $1 - \pi/6 \approx 0.4764$.
  5. With z = u, $z$ is uniform on $[0, 1)$ — only the northern hemisphere. Computing $r = \sqrt{1 - z^2}$ still works for those $z$, and $\phi$ is unchanged. The visible result: every star sits in the upper half of the sky. Looking down the $-z$ axis you see no stars at all. The sky is half-empty in a very specific orientation.
  6. For uniform sampling inside the unit ball, the volume element in spherical coordinates is $dV = r^2 \sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi$. Integrate over angles to get the marginal density of $r$: $f_r(r) \propto r^2$ on $[0, 1]$. The CDF is $F_r(r) = r^3$, so $F_r^{-1}(w) = w^{1/3}$. Recipe: sample direction by the surface method, then radius as Math.cbrt(Math.random()). Naive $r = w$ would over-cluster points near the origin.
  7. With density proportional to $\sin^k\theta$ on the surface, the marginal density of $\theta$ on the sphere becomes $\propto \sin^{k+1}\theta$ (extra $\sin\theta$ from $dA$). Substitute $z = \cos\theta$: $\sin^{k+1}\theta \, d\theta = (1 - z^2)^{k/2} \cdot |dz|$, so the marginal density of $z$ is $f_z(z) \propto (1 - z^2)^{k/2}$. The CDF has no closed form for general $k$; you would invert it numerically. For the special case $k = 2$, $f_z(z) \propto (1 - z^2)$, $F_z(z) = \frac{3}{4}(z - z^3/3) + \frac{1}{2}$ on $[-1, 1]$ (after normalizing), and you can solve the cubic for the inverse.
