Guide 12 — Adaptive Camera Transitions

Forward + up angles, regime selection via smoothstep, Early-Look vs Orbit, and timeline composition with remap.

1. Why this matters

When the user clicks a panel in web-3d-panel-navigation, the camera has to fly from its current state (some overview perspective) to the panel-perpendicular state (looking straight at the panel from the right distance). The naive solution — lerp(from, to, t) on position and target separately — falls apart in two ways:

  • Lifeless when close. If the two states are similar, a linear lerp is mechanically straight and feels robotic. We want the camera to "look ahead" before it arrives.
  • Origin-sweeping when far. If states A and B are on opposite sides of the scene (e.g. one panel is at $+Z$, the other at $+X$), lerping the position passes the camera through the origin. Visually, you swoop into the middle of the scene and back out. Disorienting.

The project's solution is a two-strategy adaptive blend. It lives in src/camera-transitions.ts:41-162 (applyTransition, calculateTransitionState, calculateEarlyLookState, calculateOrbitState) and is invoked by src/zoom-navigation.ts:200-218 (zoom-in) and :331-349 (zoom-out). The pipeline:

  1. Measure how much reorientation is needed (forward angle + up angle).
  2. Pick a regime: Early Look (slide and lock-on) for small reorientations, Orbit (sweep around a midpoint) for large ones.
  3. Smooth-blend between the two regimes in a narrow transition zone ($30°$–$35°$ total angle).
  4. Compose this with a clip-rect animation on a global timeline using remap.
This guide assumes you have guides 01 (vectors / dot product), 02 (clamp-then-acos for angles), 03 (lerp, smoothstep, easing curves, remap), and 10 (slerp on direction vectors with the parallel fallback) under your belt. We will cite them rather than re-derive.

2. Building intuition

Picture two camera states from above (top-down, $XZ$ plane):

  • State A: camera at $\mathbf{p}_A$, looking at panel target $\mathbf{t}_A$, with up $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_A$.
  • State B: camera at $\mathbf{p}_B$, looking at $\mathbf{t}_B$, with up $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_B$.

The total reorientation angle is the sum of:

  • $\theta_{\text{fwd}}$: angle between the two forward (look-at minus position) vectors. Captures "how much do I have to turn left/right/up to face the new target?"
  • $\theta_{\text{up}}$: angle between the two up vectors. Captures roll/tilt that the forward angle alone misses (e.g. a panel that's roll-rotated relative to overview).

Why sum them rather than take a max or a magnitude? Because either one being large is a strong signal that the two states feel "far apart" perceptually. The sum is a cheap, monotone scalar that grows when either angle grows. It does not need to be physically meaningful — it just needs to be a regime selector.

Now the regime decisions:

  • Total small ($\leq 30°$): the two states are close. A straight-line position lerp doesn't sweep through the origin (the path is short anyway). Use Early Look.
  • Total large ($\geq 35°$): the two states are far apart. A straight lerp would scoop through the middle of the scene. Use Orbit: keep the camera roughly the same distance from the (moving) target by slerping its offset.
  • In between (transition zone, $30°$ to $35°$): blend the two strategies componentwise so the cutover is invisible.

3. The math

3.1 Forward angle

The camera's forward direction is the unit vector from its position to its target:

$$ \hat{\mathbf{f}} = \operatorname{normalize}(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}). $$

The angle between two such forwards uses the clamp-then-acos pattern from guide 02 (clamping protects against floating-point dot products that drift outside $[-1, 1]$):

$$ \theta_{\text{fwd}} = \arccos\!\big(\operatorname{clamp}(\hat{\mathbf{f}}_A \cdot \hat{\mathbf{f}}_B,\ -1,\ 1)\big). $$

The TS code is line-for-line this formula:

src/camera-transitions.ts:63-67
const fromFwd = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(from.target, from.position).normalize();
const toFwd = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(to.target, to.position).normalize();
const fwdDot = THREE.MathUtils.clamp(fromFwd.dot(toFwd), -1, 1);
const fwdAngle = Math.acos(fwdDot);

3.2 Up angle

The up vector is already unit-length in the project (the camera-controller normalizes it on construction), so:

$$ \theta_{\text{up}} = \arccos\!\big(\operatorname{clamp}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_A \cdot \hat{\mathbf{u}}_B,\ -1,\ 1)\big). $$
src/camera-transitions.ts:70-71
const upDot = THREE.MathUtils.clamp(from.up.dot(to.up), -1, 1);
const upAngle = Math.acos(upDot);

3.3 Total reorientation

$$ \theta_{\text{total}} = \theta_{\text{fwd}} + \theta_{\text{up}}. $$

One line of TS:

src/camera-transitions.ts:74
const totalAngle = fwdAngle + upAngle;

3.4 Regime selection via smoothstep

Recall smoothstep from guide 03: a Hermite curve that is exactly $0$ below edge0, exactly $1$ above edge1, and a smooth $C^1$ ramp in between. The project uses thresholds $\theta_0 = 30°$ and $\theta_1 = 35°$:

$$ \text{blend} = \operatorname{smoothstep}(\theta_0,\ \theta_1,\ \theta_{\text{total}}). $$

This blend is the lerp parameter from "Early Look" (blend = 0) to "Orbit" (blend = 1).

src/camera-transitions.ts:76-79
// Blend: 0 (Early Look) at ≤30°, 1 (Orbit) at ≥35°
const lowerAngle = 30 * THREE.MathUtils.DEG2RAD;
const upperAngle = 35 * THREE.MathUtils.DEG2RAD;
const blend = smoothstep(lowerAngle, upperAngle, totalAngle);
The current shipped implementation has a hard cutover at blend < 0.5 rather than a componentwise blend (see camera-transitions.ts:81-87). Below we describe the conceptually clean "both strategies, then lerp by blend" form because it is the model the smoothstep variable was designed for, and it generalises to any componentwise blending you might want to do. Either form gives a visually smooth cutover because smoothstep's zero-derivative endpoints mean the regime is already pinned to one side at $\theta_{\text{total}} \le 30°$ and to the other at $\theta_{\text{total}} \ge 35°$.

3.5 Strategy 1 — Early Look

Used when $\theta_{\text{total}} \le \theta_0$. The idea: the look-at target arrives at the destination panel before the camera body finishes its slide. This makes the rotation feel concurrent with the translation rather than sequential, and it gives the user the sense that the camera "knows where it's going" the moment they click.

Position uses the symmetric ease:

$$ \mathbf{p}(t) = \operatorname{lerp}(\mathbf{p}_A,\ \mathbf{p}_B,\ \operatorname{easeInOutCubic}(t)). $$

Target uses an "early-arrival" remap (so it locks on at $t = 0.7$) followed by an ease-out:

$$ \tau = \min\!\left(\frac{t}{0.7},\ 1\right),\qquad \mathbf{t}(t) = \operatorname{lerp}(\mathbf{t}_A,\ \mathbf{t}_B,\ \operatorname{easeOutCubic}(\tau)). $$
src/camera-transitions.ts:101-109
const posT = easeInOutCubic(rawT);

// Target uses a faster curve — arrives at ~70% of the animation
const targetRaw = Math.min(rawT / 0.7, 1.0);
const targetT = easeOutCubic(targetRaw);

const position = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.position, to.position, posT);
const target = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.target, to.target, targetT);
const { up, fov } = lerpUpAndFov(from, to, posT);

Why different curves for position and target? The position uses easeInOutCubic because the camera is a physical-feeling object — a rigid body that should accelerate at the start and decelerate at the end. The target is a non-physical "where is the camera looking" semantic; an easeOutCubic applied to a remapped t/0.7 gives a quick rotational lock-on followed by a smooth deceleration without the slow start. The result: at $t = 0.35$ (camera barely moving), the camera is already aiming most of the way at the destination. The motion feels responsive instead of lazy.

3.6 Strategy 2 — Orbit

Used when $\theta_{\text{total}} \ge \theta_1$. The idea: keep the camera at a (lerped) distance from the (lerped) target, but rotate the offset around the midpoint using slerp.

Step by step. Let $t' = \operatorname{easeInOutCubic}(t)$. Compute the midpoint of the two targets:

$$ \mathbf{m} = \operatorname{lerp}(\mathbf{t}_A,\ \mathbf{t}_B,\ 0.5). $$

Compute each camera position as an offset from $\mathbf{m}$:

$$ \mathbf{o}_A = \mathbf{p}_A - \mathbf{m},\qquad \mathbf{o}_B = \mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{m}. $$

Lerp the radius (length) and slerp the direction (unit vector) — see guide 10:

$$ r(t') = \operatorname{lerp}(\|\mathbf{o}_A\|,\ \|\mathbf{o}_B\|,\ t'), \qquad \hat{\mathbf{d}}(t') = \operatorname{slerp}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_A,\ \hat{\mathbf{o}}_B,\ t'). $$

Reassemble:

$$ \mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{m} + r(t') \cdot \hat{\mathbf{d}}(t'). $$

The target is the simple eased lerp:

$$ \mathbf{t}(t) = \operatorname{lerp}(\mathbf{t}_A,\ \mathbf{t}_B,\ t'). $$

The slerp uses the parallel-vector fallback from guide 10. Here is the full TS:

src/camera-transitions.ts:124-158
const t = easeInOutCubic(rawT);

// Linearly interpolate the look-at target
const target = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.target, to.target, t);

// For position: slerp around the midpoint of the two targets
const midTarget = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.target, to.target, 0.5);
const fromOffset = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(from.position, midTarget);
const toOffset = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(to.position, midTarget);

// Interpolate radius
const fromLen = fromOffset.length();
const toLen = toOffset.length();
const currentLen = fromLen + (toLen - fromLen) * t;

// Slerp direction
const fromDir = fromOffset.clone().normalize();
const toDir = toOffset.clone().normalize();
const dot = THREE.MathUtils.clamp(fromDir.dot(toDir), -1, 1);
const theta = Math.acos(dot);

let position: THREE.Vector3;
if (theta < 0.001) {
  // Nearly parallel — fall back to lerp
  position = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.position, to.position, t);
} else {
  const sinTheta = Math.sin(theta);
  const a = Math.sin((1 - t) * theta) / sinTheta;
  const b = Math.sin(t * theta) / sinTheta;
  const slerpedDir = new THREE.Vector3()
    .addScaledVector(fromDir, a)
    .addScaledVector(toDir, b)
    .normalize();
  position = midTarget.clone().add(slerpedDir.multiplyScalar(currentLen));
}

3.7 Componentwise blend

Conceptually, in the transition zone $\theta_{\text{total}} \in [\theta_0, \theta_1]$ the final state is the componentwise lerp:

$$ \mathbf{p}_{\text{out}} = \operatorname{lerp}(\mathbf{p}_{\text{EL}},\ \mathbf{p}_{\text{Orbit}},\ \text{blend}), $$ $$ \mathbf{t}_{\text{out}} = \operatorname{lerp}(\mathbf{t}_{\text{EL}},\ \mathbf{t}_{\text{Orbit}},\ \text{blend}), $$

and likewise for up and FOV. Both strategies share the same lerpUpAndFov helper; the only fields that actually differ between regimes are position and target.

3.8 Phase composition with remap

The full zoom-in is two parallel timelines:

  • A camera animation of duration cameraDuration (default 1000 ms).
  • A clip-rect / opacity animation of duration clipDuration (default 400 ms) which starts late so it overlaps the tail of the camera animation by overlapRatio (default 0.15).

The total wall-clock duration is:

$$ T = \texttt{cameraDuration} + \texttt{clipDuration} \cdot (1 - \texttt{overlapRatio}). $$

The camera ends at global progress $g_{\text{camEnd}} = \texttt{cameraDuration}/T$. The clip starts at $g_{\text{clipStart}} = g_{\text{camEnd}} - \texttt{overlapRatio}\cdot\texttt{clipDuration}/T$. From a single global $g \in [0, 1]$ we extract per-phase local progresses with remap (guide 03):

$$ p_{\text{cam}} = \operatorname{remap}(g,\ 0,\ g_{\text{camEnd}}), \qquad p_{\text{clip}} = \operatorname{remap}(g,\ g_{\text{clipStart}},\ 1). $$

Both are clamped to $[0, 1]$. The TS in zoom-navigation.ts reads exactly this way:

src/zoom-navigation.ts:200-218
const { promise, cancel } = this.timeline.runSequence(totalDuration, (progress) => {
  const rawCameraT = remap(progress, 0, cameraEnd);
  this.cameraController.setToState(
    calculateTransitionState(startCameraState, targetCameraState, rawCameraT)
  );

  if (progress >= clipStart) {
    const clipRaw = remap(progress, clipStart, 1.0);
    const clipT = clipRaw * clipRaw * (3 - 2 * clipRaw);
    const currentRect = lerpScreenRect(planeRect, targetRect, clipT);
    this.clipController.setClipRect(currentRect);
    this.clipController.setOpacity(clipT);
  }

  if (progress >= clipStart) {
    const fadeT = remap(progress, clipStart, planeFadeEnd);
    this.sceneGraph.setPlaneOpacity(planeId, 1 - fadeT);
  }
});

The zoom-out (:331-349) is the same pattern with the clip phase first and the camera phase second; you'll see the "reverse" call calculateTransitionState(overviewState, startCameraState, 1 - rawCameraT).

4. In this codebase

The four code excerpts above are the project's full implementation. Walking the call:

  1. zoom-navigation.ts:200 ticks the timeline. Each tick has a global progress in $[0, 1]$.
  2. It computes rawCameraT = remap(progress, 0, cameraEnd) and feeds it to calculateTransitionState.
  3. Inside calculateTransitionState (lines 58-88), the angles get computed (3.1, 3.2, 3.3), blend gets computed via smoothstep (3.4), and either calculateEarlyLookState (3.5) or calculateOrbitState (3.6) is returned.
  4. The result is fed to cameraController.setToState, which writes position/target/up/FOV into the camera.
  5. Once progress ≥ clipStart, the clip rectangle starts animating with its own remap'd local progress.

The two remap calls are not coincidence. They are the project's general way of running multiple parallel phases on one global clock, and both come from src/easing.ts:34.

6. Worked examples

Example 1 — orthogonal panels (Orbit regime)

State A: $\mathbf{p}_A = (0, 0, 1500)$, $\mathbf{t}_A = (0, 0, 0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_A = (0, 1, 0)$. State B: $\mathbf{p}_B = (1500, 0, 0)$, $\mathbf{t}_B = (0, 0, 0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_B = (0, 1, 0)$. Compute $\theta_{\text{fwd}}$, $\theta_{\text{up}}$, $\theta_{\text{total}}$, the regime / blend, and the camera position at $t = 0.5$.

Forward angle. $\hat{\mathbf{f}}_A = \operatorname{normalize}((0,0,0) - (0,0,1500)) = (0, 0, -1)$. $\hat{\mathbf{f}}_B = (-1, 0, 0)$. Dot product $= 0$. So $\theta_{\text{fwd}} = \arccos(0) = 90°$.

Up angle. $(0,1,0) \cdot (0,1,0) = 1$, so $\theta_{\text{up}} = 0°$.

Total. $\theta_{\text{total}} = 90°$, well above $\theta_1 = 35°$, so $\text{blend} = 1$ → pure Orbit.

Position at $t = 0.5$. $t' = \operatorname{easeInOutCubic}(0.5) = 0.5$. Midpoint $\mathbf{m} = (0,0,0)$. Offsets $\mathbf{o}_A = (0,0,1500)$, $\mathbf{o}_B = (1500,0,0)$. Lengths both $1500$, so $r = 1500$. Directions $(0,0,1)$ and $(1,0,0)$, dot $0$, so $\theta = 90°$, $\sin\theta = 1$. With $t' = 0.5$, $a = b = \sin(45°) = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. Slerped direction $= (\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2})$, then normalized (already unit). Position $= 1500 \cdot (\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx (1060.66, 0, 1060.66)$.

Note how this stays at radius 1500 from the origin — a Lerp of the positions would give $(750, 0, 750)$ at radius $\approx 1060$, scooping inward. Orbit holds the radius; lerp does not.

Example 2 — small reorientation (Early Look regime)

State A: $\mathbf{p}_A = (0, 0, 1500)$, $\mathbf{t}_A = (0, 0, 0)$. State B: $\mathbf{p}_B = (100, 0, 1490)$, $\mathbf{t}_B = (50, 0, 0)$. Up vectors equal.

$\hat{\mathbf{f}}_A = (0,0,-1)$. $\mathbf{t}_B - \mathbf{p}_B = (-50, 0, -1490)$, length $\approx 1490.84$, so $\hat{\mathbf{f}}_B \approx (-0.0335, 0, -0.9994)$. Dot $\approx 0.9994$, so $\theta_{\text{fwd}} \approx \arccos(0.9994) \approx 1.93°$. Up angle $0°$. Total $\approx 1.93°$, well below $\theta_0 = 30°$, so $\text{blend} = 0$ → pure Early Look.

Position at $t = 0.5$: $\operatorname{easeInOutCubic}(0.5) = 0.5$, so $\mathbf{p}(0.5) = (50, 0, 1495)$. Target at $t = 0.5$: $\tau = \min(0.5/0.7, 1) = 0.714$, $\operatorname{easeOutCubic}(0.714) = 1 - (1 - 0.714)^3 = 1 - 0.0234 = 0.977$, so $\mathbf{t}(0.5) = \operatorname{lerp}((0,0,0), (50,0,0), 0.977) = (48.84, 0, 0)$. The target is essentially already at its destination while the camera is still halfway there. That is the "early lock-on" effect.

Example 3 — in the transition zone

Take a configuration with $\theta_{\text{fwd}} = 25°$, $\theta_{\text{up}} = 7°$. Then $\theta_{\text{total}} = 32°$. With $\theta_0 = 30°$, $\theta_1 = 35°$:

Normalize: $u = (32 - 30)/(35 - 30) = 0.4$. Smoothstep: $u^2(3 - 2u) = 0.16 \cdot 2.2 = 0.352$. So $\text{blend} \approx 0.352$, meaning the output is $\approx 65\%$ Early-Look-like and $\approx 35\%$ Orbit-like.

Appendix — solutions

1. Smoothstep at $31°, 33°, 34.5°$

$u(31°) = 0.2$, smoothstep $= 0.04 \cdot 2.6 = 0.104$.
$u(33°) = 0.6$, smoothstep $= 0.36 \cdot 1.8 = 0.648$.
$u(34.5°) = 0.9$, smoothstep $= 0.81 \cdot 1.2 = 0.972$.

2. Forward angle from $(1,0,0)$ to $(0.7071, 0, -0.7071)$

Dot product $= 0.7071$. $\arccos(0.7071) = 45°$.

3. Why different easing for position vs target

Position is a physical-feeling quantity: the camera is a "thing" with momentum, so a symmetric ease-in-out (slow start, slow end) is what users expect from anything that visibly moves. The target is a non-physical semantic — it's "where the lens is aimed." If position used the same t/0.7 early-arrival remap, the camera body would race the first $70\%$ of the way and then sit for the remaining $30\%$, which looks broken. By keeping the position on a symmetric ease and only racing the target, the user sees the camera immediately commit visually (rotation locks on early) while the body still slides in at a calm physical pace.

4. The slerp fallback

It triggers when $\hat{\mathbf{o}}_A$ and $\hat{\mathbf{o}}_B$ are nearly parallel — for example, two states with the same camera-to-target direction but different distances (e.g. $\mathbf{p}_A = (0,0,1500)$ and $\mathbf{p}_B = (0,0,1200)$ both looking at the origin). Then $\theta \to 0$, $\sin\theta \to 0$, and the formula sin((1-t)θ)/sinθ divides $0/0$. Without the fallback you get NaNs and the camera teleports; with it, the code does a plain lerp which is exactly the geometric limit anyway. See guide 10 for the full slerp derivation.

5. Smoothstep zone $60°$–$65°$ for a $45°$ transition

$45° < 60°$, so $\text{blend} = 0$ → pure Early Look. The user would see the camera lerp linearly through the scene origin instead of orbiting around it. Visually: the camera scoops into the middle of the scene before continuing on to the destination panel, possibly clipping into other panels along the way. This is the disorientation the project specifically engineered Orbit to avoid.

6. Why the hard cutover still feels smooth

Smoothstep has zero derivative at both endpoints. Near $\theta_0$ the blend value is approaching $0$ smoothly, and near $\theta_1$ it is approaching $1$ smoothly, so the strategies you're cutting between are themselves nearly identical at the cutover point — the EL state and the Orbit state are not wildly different when the angle is borderline. The visual cutover shows up only as a small change in path curvature, not a teleport. (For applications where even that subtle change is unacceptable, the componentwise blend in §3.7 is the principled answer.)

7. Phase composition arithmetic

$T = 1000 + 400 \cdot 0.85 = 1340$ ms. $g_{\text{camEnd}} = 1000/1340 \approx 0.7463$. $g_{\text{clipStart}} = 0.7463 - (0.15 \cdot 400/1340) = 0.7463 - 0.0448 \approx 0.7015$. The clip phase begins at $g_{\text{clipStart}} \cdot T \approx 0.7015 \cdot 1340 \approx 940$ ms after the animation starts. (Sanity check: the clip phase runs from $\approx 940$ ms to $1340$ ms, a duration of $400$ ms — exactly clipDuration.)
