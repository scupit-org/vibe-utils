Guide 10 — Spherical Linear Interpolation (slerp)

Tenth in an ordered curriculum. Earlier guides cover vectors and the dot product (1), trig and the arccos safety clamp (2), lerp and easings (3), and quaternions on the 4-sphere (5).

1. Why this matters

Open src/camera-transitions.ts and scroll to calculateOrbitState, lines 119–162. Inside the else branch on lines 149–157 you will find the heart of the orbit camera transition: a hand-rolled slerp on the camera-position offset from a midpoint. That call is what makes the camera sweep on a smooth curved arc when navigating between two panels facing different directions, instead of slicing through the middle of the scene.

Why does this matter? When you reorient between two camera states with different positions and look-directions, naive componentwise lerp on the positions traces a straight chord through 3-space. If the two camera positions are roughly the same distance from the focus point but on opposite sides of it, the chord passes through (or near) the focus — your camera dives toward the scene origin and then climbs back out. Slerp on the offset from a shared midpoint instead keeps the camera on a smooth arc around the focus at roughly constant distance. The user perceives a turntable / orbital sweep instead of an unsettling lunge.

The blend regime in calculateTransitionState (lines 41–88) decides whether to call this orbit branch at all: only when the total reorientation angle exceeds about 35°. Below that the camera uses Early-Look (a straight-line lerp) instead. Guide 12 will cover the regime selector; this guide is about the math inside the orbit branch.

2. Building intuition

Forget cameras for a moment. Picture two unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ in the plane, both anchored at the origin, both with length 1. Their tips sit on the unit circle. There are two natural paths from the tip of $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ to the tip of $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$:

  • The chord — a straight line across the inside of the circle. This is the path traced by the naive average $\operatorname{lerp}(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}, t) = (1-t)\hat{\mathbf{a}} + t\hat{\mathbf{b}}$.
  • The arc — the curve that stays on the unit circle the whole way. This is the path traced by slerp.

The chord has two problems. First, its length isn't 1: at $t = 0.5$ with $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ separated by 90°, the midpoint is

$$\left\|\tfrac{\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}}{2}\right\| = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}})\cdot (\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}})} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 + 2\cos\theta + 1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{2 + 2\cos 90^\circ} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \approx 0.707.$$

The vector dips inward — it's shorter than 1. If you renormalize after the lerp you get back onto the circle, but you don't get there at constant angular speed: the renormalized point creeps near the endpoints and hurries through the middle.

The arc avoids both problems. It stays on the circle (length always 1) and it travels at constant angular speed. That second property is the one slerp gets right that lerp-then-normalize does not.

Slogan. Lerp connects two points by the shortest line. Slerp connects two unit-length points by the shortest curve that stays on the unit sphere, traversed at constant angular speed.

3. The math

3.1 The problem

Given two unit vectors $\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}} \in \mathbb{R}^n$, find a parametric path $\mathbf{p}: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with

  1. $\mathbf{p}(0) = \hat{\mathbf{a}}$, $\mathbf{p}(1) = \hat{\mathbf{b}}$ (matches endpoints),
  2. $\|\mathbf{p}(t)\| = 1$ for every $t$ (stays on the unit sphere),
  3. the angle $\angle(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \mathbf{p}(t))$ grows linearly in $t$ (constant angular speed).

Condition (3) is the one that picks out a unique curve from all the length-preserving paths.

3.2 Setup

Let $\theta = \arccos(\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}})$ be the angle between the two vectors (recall from guide 1 that for unit vectors, the dot product equals the cosine of their angle; recall from guide 2 that we clamp the dot product to $[-1, 1]$ before passing it to Math.acos to guard against floating-point overshoot).

We will look for $\mathbf{p}(t)$ as a linear combination of just the two endpoints,

$$\mathbf{p}(t) = \alpha(t)\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \beta(t)\hat{\mathbf{b}},$$

where $\alpha, \beta : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ are scalar functions to be determined. Why is this the right ansatz? Because the great-circle arc from $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ to $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ lies entirely in the 2D plane spanned by $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$, and any vector in that plane can be written as a linear combination of those two basis vectors.

3.3 Derivation

From condition (3), the angle from $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ to $\mathbf{p}(t)$ should be exactly $t\theta$. Project $\mathbf{p}(t)$ onto $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ using the dot product. Since both $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\mathbf{p}(t)$ are unit vectors:

$$\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \cos(t\theta). \tag{i}$$

By symmetry, the angle from $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ to $\mathbf{p}(t)$ is $(1 - t)\theta$ (the remaining part of the arc), so

$$\hat{\mathbf{b}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \cos((1 - t)\theta). \tag{ii}$$

Now expand the left-hand sides using the ansatz $\mathbf{p}(t) = \alpha\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \beta\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ and the identities $\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{a}} = 1$, $\hat{\mathbf{b}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = 1$, $\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = \cos\theta$:

$$\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \alpha \cdot 1 + \beta \cos\theta = \cos(t\theta),$$

$$\hat{\mathbf{b}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \alpha \cos\theta + \beta \cdot 1 = \cos((1-t)\theta).$$

That is a 2×2 linear system in $(\alpha, \beta)$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \cos\theta \\ \cos\theta & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(t\theta) \\ \cos((1-t)\theta) \end{pmatrix}.$$

The determinant is $1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta$, which is nonzero whenever $\theta \notin \{0, \pi\}$. Solving via Cramer's rule:

$$\alpha = \frac{\cos(t\theta) - \cos\theta\cos((1-t)\theta)}{\sin^2\theta}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\cos((1-t)\theta) - \cos\theta\cos(t\theta)}{\sin^2\theta}.$$

Both numerators simplify with the product-to-sum identity $\cos A \cos B = \tfrac{1}{2}[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)]$. For $\alpha$:

$$\cos(t\theta) - \cos\theta\cos((1-t)\theta) = \cos(t\theta) - \tfrac{1}{2}[\cos(t\theta) + \cos((2 - t)\theta - \theta)]$$

which after carefully bookkeeping the angles collapses to $\sin\theta \sin((1-t)\theta)$. (A more direct route is to guess $\alpha = \sin((1-t)\theta)/\sin\theta$ and verify by substitution; that verification is the cleanest path and we walk through it next.)

The simplified solution is

$$\boxed{\;\alpha(t) = \frac{\sin((1-t)\theta)}{\sin\theta}, \qquad \beta(t) = \frac{\sin(t\theta)}{\sin\theta}.\;}$$

3.4 The slerp formula

$$\operatorname{slerp}(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}, t) = \frac{\sin((1-t)\theta)}{\sin\theta}\, \hat{\mathbf{a}} + \frac{\sin(t\theta)}{\sin\theta}\, \hat{\mathbf{b}}.$$

And in the project's TypeScript (lines 150–156):

const sinTheta = Math.sin(theta);
const a = Math.sin((1 - t) * theta) / sinTheta;
const b = Math.sin(t * theta) / sinTheta;
const slerpedDir = new THREE.Vector3()
  .addScaledVector(fromDir, a)
  .addScaledVector(toDir, b)
  .normalize();

Each TS line corresponds to one piece of the formula. The trailing .normalize() is a paranoia step against floating-point drift — mathematically the result is already unit-length.

3.5 Verify the formula

Endpoint at $t = 0$: $\sin((1 - 0)\theta) = \sin\theta$ and $\sin(0 \cdot \theta) = 0$, so $\alpha(0) = 1$, $\beta(0) = 0$, giving $\operatorname{slerp}(\cdot, \cdot, 0) = \hat{\mathbf{a}}$. Good.

Endpoint at $t = 1$: $\sin(0) = 0$ and $\sin\theta / \sin\theta = 1$, giving $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$. Good.

Midpoint at $t = 0.5$ with $\theta = 90°$: $\sin(0.5 \cdot 90°) = \sin(45°) = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and $\sin(90°) = 1$, so $\alpha = \beta = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, and

$$\operatorname{slerp} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(\hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}}).$$

That's exactly what you'd get from $\operatorname{lerp}(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}, 0.5)$ followed by normalization in the special case $t = 0.5$ — the two methods agree at the midpoint when the angle is 90°. They disagree at every other $t$, which we'll see in the widget.

3.6 Constant angular speed

The point of construction was that $\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \cos(t\theta)$. So the angle from $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ to $\mathbf{p}(t)$ is

$$\arccos(\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t)) = \arccos(\cos(t\theta)) = t\theta.$$

That is linear in $t$. When $t$ moves from 0 to 0.1 the angular position moves $0.1\theta$, and when it moves from 0.5 to 0.6 it again moves $0.1\theta$. Equal slices of $t$ produce equal slices of arc.

By contrast, lerp-then-normalize sends $t = 0.1$ to a renormalized chord point whose angle from $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ is not $0.1\theta$ — it lags near the endpoints and races through the middle. The widget below makes this visible by tick spacing.

3.7 The parallel-vector fallback

If $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ are parallel (or nearly so), $\theta \to 0$ and $\sin\theta \to 0$, so the slerp formula becomes $0/0$. The project guards against this on line 146:

let position: THREE.Vector3;
if (theta < 0.001) {
  // Nearly parallel — fall back to lerp
  position = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.position, to.position, t);
} else {
  // ... slerp branch ...
}

Why is plain lerp acceptable here? Because when the angle is tiny, the chord and the arc are nearly identical: they share the same endpoints, and the maximum chord-to-arc deviation (the sagitta, $1 - \cos(\theta/2) \approx \theta^2/8$) grows like $\theta^2$. With $\theta < 0.001$ rad, the chord deviation is below $1.25 \times 10^{-7}$ on a unit sphere — comfortably sub-pixel for any realistic camera distance. Geometrically, lerp is the first-order Taylor expansion of slerp in $\theta$, so the fallback is the correct linear approximation rather than a hack.

Antipodal case ($\theta = \pi$). If $\hat{\mathbf{a}} = -\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ then $\sin\theta = \sin\pi = 0$ again — and now the two vectors don't define a unique arc (any great circle through them works). Three.js's Quaternion.slerp handles antipodal quaternions by flipping the sign of one operand to take the short way. The vector slerp in camera-transitions.ts doesn't bother because in panel navigation the camera's offset from the midpoint is never exactly opposite between two panel views — you don't end up looking at the same target from exactly the back of where you started.

3.8 Slerp on quaternions vs. slerp on vectors

The slerp in camera-transitions.ts operates on direction vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, not on quaternions. The math is literally the same formula: it just lives on a different sphere. Direction vectors live on the unit 2-sphere $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$; unit quaternions live on the unit 3-sphere $S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4$ (covered in guide 5). The slerp formula doesn't care about ambient dimension — it only cares that the two operands are unit-length and that their dot product gives $\cos\theta$.

Caveat for quaternions. When you slerp two unit quaternions, the operands' dot product is $\cos(\theta/2)$, not $\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the rotation angle the quaternions represent. (This is the half-angle convention from guide 5: a unit quaternion $q = [\cos(\theta/2), \sin(\theta/2)\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}]$ encodes a $\theta$-rotation about $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$.) The slerp formula itself still works unchanged — but if you read the "angle" out of the dot product expecting the rotation angle, double it. Three.js's Quaternion.slerp handles this internally and additionally flips the sign of one operand if the dot is negative, taking the short path around the 720° double-cover. Guide 5 derives why $q$ and $-q$ represent the same rotation.

One thing to flag: the orbit branch slerps the camera's position-offset direction, but the camera's up vector is interpolated separately by lerpUpAndFov (line 15) using plain lerp followed by normalize. For typical small up-vector tilts (the up vector usually doesn't change much between panels) the chord deviation is negligible, so the cheaper lerp+normalize is fine. If the project ever needed to rotate up by large angles, that line would want to become a slerp too.

4. In this codebase

Let's now read calculateOrbitState end-to-end with the math we have. Lines 119–162:

function calculateOrbitState(
  from: CameraState,
  to: CameraState,
  rawT: number
): CameraState {
  const t = easeInOutCubic(rawT);

  // Linearly interpolate the look-at target
  const target = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.target, to.target, t);

  // For position: slerp around the midpoint of the two targets
  const midTarget = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.target, to.target, 0.5);
  const fromOffset = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(from.position, midTarget);
  const toOffset = new THREE.Vector3().subVectors(to.position, midTarget);

  // Interpolate radius
  const fromLen = fromOffset.length();
  const toLen = toOffset.length();
  const currentLen = fromLen + (toLen - fromLen) * t;

  // Slerp direction
  const fromDir = fromOffset.clone().normalize();
  const toDir = toOffset.clone().normalize();
  const dot = THREE.MathUtils.clamp(fromDir.dot(toDir), -1, 1);
  const theta = Math.acos(dot);

  let position: THREE.Vector3;
  if (theta < 0.001) {
    // Nearly parallel — fall back to lerp
    position = new THREE.Vector3().lerpVectors(from.position, to.position, t);
  } else {
    const sinTheta = Math.sin(theta);
    const a = Math.sin((1 - t) * theta) / sinTheta;
    const b = Math.sin(t * theta) / sinTheta;
    const slerpedDir = new THREE.Vector3()
      .addScaledVector(fromDir, a)
      .addScaledVector(toDir, b)
      .normalize();
    position = midTarget.clone().add(slerpedDir.multiplyScalar(currentLen));
  }

  const { up, fov } = lerpUpAndFov(from, to, t);
  return { position, target, up, fov };
}

4.1 The midpoint trick

Slerp acts on a unit sphere centered at the origin. But the camera's positions aren't unit-length and aren't centered at the origin — they're scattered in world-space. The fix is to change frame: pick a center $\mathbf{m}$ (here, the midpoint of from.target and to.target), measure both camera positions relative to $\mathbf{m}$ as offsets, slerp those offsets as direction vectors, then add $\mathbf{m}$ back at the end.

$$\mathbf{m} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{t}_\text{from} + \mathbf{t}_\text{to}),$$

$$\mathbf{o}_\text{from} = \mathbf{p}_\text{from} - \mathbf{m},\qquad \mathbf{o}_\text{to} = \mathbf{p}_\text{to} - \mathbf{m}.$$

The midpoint of the two look-at targets is a sensible pivot because it sits roughly "in front of" both cameras — the camera is going to stay oriented around scene content there, so a sphere centered at $\mathbf{m}$ captures the geometry that matters.

4.2 Splitting direction from radius

The two offsets generally don't have the same length: fromLen and toLen may differ. So the path can't simply lie on a single sphere. The code splits the problem:

  • Direction — slerped, so the unit-length direction travels at constant angular speed along the great-circle arc.
  • Radius — lerped scalar-wise: $r(t) = r_\text{from} + (r_\text{to} - r_\text{from}) \cdot t$.

The code:

const fromLen = fromOffset.length();
const toLen   = toOffset.length();
const currentLen = fromLen + (toLen - fromLen) * t;
// ...
position = midTarget.clone().add(slerpedDir.multiplyScalar(currentLen));

Mathematically:

$$\mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{m} + r(t) \cdot \operatorname{slerp}\!\left( \hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{from}, \hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{to}, t\right).$$

The result is a position that orbits $\mathbf{m}$ at constant angular speed while smoothly approaching or receding in radius. It's an Archimedean spiral arc on a cone if $r_\text{from} \ne r_\text{to}$ (angle linear in $t$, radius linear in $t$ — both arithmetic, not exponential), and a perfect circular arc if they're equal.

4.3 The $t$ that gets passed in is already eased

Note line 124: const t = easeInOutCubic(rawT);. The slerp's $t$ is the eased animation parameter, not the linear animation clock. So the camera doesn't traverse the arc at constant angular speed in wall-clock time — it eases in and out. Constant-angular-speed in $t$ plus an ease-in-out curve in $t(\text{wall time})$ gives a result that slows down at both ends, which is what feels right for a UI transition.

6. Worked examples

Example A — orthogonal vectors at $t = 0.5$

Let $\hat{\mathbf{a}} = (1, 0, 0)$ and $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = (0, 1, 0)$. Then $\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = 0$, so $\theta = 90° = \pi/2$. At $t = 0.5$:

$$\alpha = \frac{\sin(0.5 \cdot \pi/2)}{\sin(\pi/2)} = \frac{\sin(\pi/4)}{1} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\sin(0.5 \cdot \pi/2)}{\sin(\pi/2)} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}.$$

$$\operatorname{slerp} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(1, 0, 0) + \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(0, 1, 0) = \left(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0\right) \approx (0.707, 0.707, 0).$$

Magnitude: $\sqrt{0.5 + 0.5} = 1$. Good. Compare to lerp: $\operatorname{lerp}(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}, 0.5) = (0.5, 0.5, 0)$, magnitude $\sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.707$. Lerp-then-normalize gives back the same $(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0)$ — agreeing with slerp at the midpoint when the angle is 90°.

Example B — orthogonal vectors at $t = 0.25$ (where they disagree)

Same $\hat{\mathbf{a}}, \hat{\mathbf{b}}, \theta$ as above. At $t = 0.25$:

$\alpha = \sin(0.75 \cdot \pi/2) / 1 = \sin(67.5°) \approx 0.9239.$
$\beta = \sin(0.25 \cdot \pi/2) / 1 = \sin(22.5°) \approx 0.3827.$

$$\operatorname{slerp} \approx (0.9239, 0.3827, 0).$$

Magnitude: $\sqrt{0.9239^2 + 0.3827^2} = \sqrt{0.8536 + 0.1464} = 1$. Good. Angle from $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$: $\arccos(0.9239) = 22.5°$. That is exactly $0.25 \cdot 90°$. Constant angular speed confirmed.

Now compare to lerp+normalize. Bare lerp: $(0.75, 0.25, 0)$, magnitude $\sqrt{0.625} \approx 0.7906$. Normalize: $(0.9487, 0.3162, 0)$. Angle from $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$: $\arccos(0.9487) \approx 18.43°$. Not 22.5° — it lags by about 4°. That lag is what would show up as a non-uniform camera-orbit speed if the project used lerp+normalize instead of slerp.

Example C — orbit-style use

Suppose from.position = (5, 0, 0), from.target = (0, 0, 0), and to.position = (0, 0, 5), to.target = (0, 0, 0) (so both look at the origin from 5 units out). The midpoint of the targets is $\mathbf{m} = (0, 0, 0)$. The two offsets are $\mathbf{o}_\text{from} = (5, 0, 0)$ and $\mathbf{o}_\text{to} = (0, 0, 5)$, both of length 5.

Normalized: $\hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{from} = (1, 0, 0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{to} = (0, 0, 1)$. Their dot product is 0, so $\theta = 90°$.

Radius: $r(t) = 5 + (5 - 5) t = 5$ — constant at 5.

At $t = 0.5$: $\operatorname{slerp}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{from}, \hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{to}, 0.5) = (\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2})$. The camera position is

$$\mathbf{p}(0.5) = \mathbf{m} + 5 \cdot (\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, 0, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}) \approx (3.54, 0, 3.54).$$

Distance from origin: 5. The camera traced a quarter-circle in the $xz$-plane at constant radius. Compare to plain lerp on positions: $\operatorname{lerp}((5, 0, 0), (0, 0, 5), 0.5) = (2.5, 0, 2.5)$, distance $\sqrt{12.5} \approx 3.54$. The plain-lerp midpoint is much closer to the origin — it would dive halfway into the scene.

Solution 1.

$\theta = \pi/2$. $\alpha = \sin(0.75 \pi/2) = \sin(67.5°) \approx 0.9239$. $\beta = \sin(0.25\pi/2) = \sin(22.5°) \approx 0.3827$. Result $(0.9239, 0.3827)$. Magnitude $\sqrt{0.9239^2 + 0.3827^2} = 1$. Angle from $(1, 0)$: $\arccos(0.9239) = 22.5°$.

Solution 2.

$\hat{\mathbf{a}} = (1, 0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = (-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2})$, $\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = -1/2$, so $\theta = 120°$. At $t = 0.5$: $\alpha = \beta = \sin(60°)/\sin(120°) = (\sqrt 3/2)/(\sqrt 3/2) = 1$. So slerp $= \hat{\mathbf{a}} + \hat{\mathbf{b}} = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2})$. Magnitude $\sqrt{1/4 + 3/4} = 1$. Angle from $(1, 0)$: $\arccos(1/2) = 60° = 0.5 \theta$.

Solution 3.

$\theta = \arccos(0.9) \approx 0.4510$ rad $\approx 25.84°$. That is far above the $0.001$ rad threshold, so the slerp branch runs.

Solution 4.

$\theta = \pi/3$. $\alpha = \sin((1 - 1/3)\pi/3) / \sin(\pi/3) = \sin(40°)/\sin(60°) \approx 0.6428 / 0.8660 \approx 0.7422$. $\beta = \sin((1/3)\pi/3)/\sin(\pi/3) = \sin(20°)/\sin(60°) \approx 0.3420/0.8660 \approx 0.3949$.

Solution 5.

$\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \alpha(t) \hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{a}} + \beta(t) \hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = \alpha(t) + \beta(t) \cos\theta$. Substitute $\alpha, \beta$:

$\hat{\mathbf{a}} \cdot \mathbf{p}(t) = \frac{\sin((1-t)\theta) + \sin(t\theta)\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$. Expand $\sin((1-t)\theta) = \sin\theta \cos(t\theta) - \cos\theta \sin(t\theta)$. So the numerator becomes $\sin\theta \cos(t\theta) - \cos\theta \sin(t\theta) + \sin(t\theta) \cos\theta = \sin\theta \cos(t\theta)$. Divide by $\sin\theta$: $\cos(t\theta)$. Done.

Solution 6.

Lerp+normalize spaces points uniformly along the chord and then projects them outward to the circle. Equal chord-distance does not produce equal arc-distance: the chord parameterization is denser near the endpoints (when projected back to the circle) and sparser in the middle. Slerp is defined by equal arc-distance directly. They only coincide at $t = 0$, $t = 1$, and at $t = 0.5$ when the chord midpoint already lies on the perpendicular bisector that hits the arc midpoint — which happens automatically by symmetry whenever you sample the midpoint of any symmetric configuration.

Solution 7.

The two camera positions are roughly equidistant from the look-at midpoint but opposite each other. Plain lerp traces the chord between them, which passes through or very near the look-at midpoint — the scene center. So at $t \approx 0.5$ the camera would be sitting on top of the focus point: the user would see the camera dive into the scene, the field of view fill with extreme close-ups or clip through geometry, then climb back out to the destination. The slerp branch keeps the camera at constant radius (since both offsets are the same length) and sweeps it around, which is what the user expects for an orbit.

Solution 8.

For a chord subtending angle $\theta$ on a unit circle, the sagitta (maximum perpendicular distance from the chord to the arc) is $1 - \cos(\theta/2) \approx \theta^2/8$ for small $\theta$. With $\theta = 0.001$, the deviation is about $1.25 \times 10^{-7}$ on a unit circle. Scaling to a camera offset of length 10 gives about $1.25 \times 10^{-6}$ world units — far below sub-pixel for any realistic projection.

Solution 9.

Replace $r(t) = r_\text{from} + (r_\text{to} - r_\text{from}) t$ with $r(t) = r_\text{from} + (r_\text{to} - r_\text{from})\, S(t)$ where $S(t) = t^2(3 - 2t)$ is smoothstep. Position becomes $\mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{m} + r(t)\,\operatorname{slerp}(\hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{from}, \hat{\mathbf{o}}_\text{to}, t)$. Qualitatively: the camera would dwell at $r = 2$ near the start of the orbit, accelerate outward through the middle, and dwell at $r = 8$ near the end. The angular sweep would still be constant-speed in $t$, but the radial expansion would feel "held" at both endpoints. Whether that feels nicer or worse depends on context — one issue: the radius-easing and the eased animation $t = \texttt{easeInOutCubic}(\texttt{rawT})$ already in line 124 would compose, possibly over-easing.
